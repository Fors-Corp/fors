# State-of-the-art survey and tension analysis (2026-09-19)

> Web research by five survey agents, cross-examined by one adversarial analyst. Claims dated after mid-2026 were not independently re-verified; check sources before relying on them.

## Feasibility verdict

Blunt verdict: the four headline goals as stated are not simultaneously achievable, and two of them are not achievable at all. They are also not equally valuable, so the right move is to trade three of them down and win the fourth outright.

What is genuinely winnable, solo, with AI coding agents, in roughly 12-18 months of sustained work (~100-200k lines): goal 3, the fastest compiler. It is an architecture-and-discipline outcome, not a research outcome. The mechanisms are all known and individually proven: no textual include, mandatory acyclic module graph, imports-first, flat SoA index-referenced IR, per-declaration content-hash query DAG, bounded local type inference, shape-based generics, a copy-and-patch dev tier, an incrementally patchable object format. A sub-50ms p95 single-token-edit rebuild including link, and a 3x-over-Go cold debug build, are reachable targets. Goal 2, security built in, is also largely winnable in the same window as a design property rather than a research program — parameter-convention aliasing plus affine ownership, trap-everywhere-no-UB semantics, module-declared capabilities checked on the module graph, a pure deterministic comptime with declared inputs, and a declarative sandboxed build system with signed reproducible packages. The capability-confined build system is the single most differentiated and most under-served thing in this entire plan: no mainstream toolchain does it, the xz attack is the argument, and it is cheap relative to its value.

What needs a team and years: a release backend competitive with LLVM (a decade of person-years; take the LLVM dependency and accept that 'fastest compiler' is a debug/incremental claim, stated as such); an MLIR-class multi-level IR with an autoscheduler (Modular's funded team, now Qualcomm's); multi-vendor GPU codegen; a PGAS/distributed runtime (Chapel took DARPA money and two decades to reach C/C++ parity claims); SMT verification infrastructure; formal soundness proofs; Windows/macOS incremental linking; and stdlib/ecosystem breadth. Goal 1 lands at parity-not-supremacy: within 5% geomean of best-of-C/Rust is a realistic 3-5 year outcome with targeted 1.3-4x wins on layout- and vectorization-sensitive kernels, which is a real and defensible claim. 'Most efficient in existence' is not falsifiable and should be retired from the vocabulary now, before it gets published.

Goal 4 as stated is dead on arrival and should be abandoned explicitly rather than quietly. Peak parallel performance on current accelerators requires warp specialization, TMA and newest-generation tensor-core instructions; the measured state of the art is that Triton trails CuTe by ~2.7x on FlashAttention-4-class kernels on B200 and sustains 62-101% of cuBLAS on GEMM, and the top of the TOP500 (LineShine, 2.198 EF/s, June 2026) is won by custom silicon, a proprietary interconnect and OS co-design. A language cannot beat hardware it does not control, and the one player with a plausible single-source portable story just became a chip vendor's subsidiary.

Sharpest realistic positioning: the fastest-building memory-safe systems language, with capability-confined reproducible builds and first-class structured CPU parallelism. Three claims, each measurable, each currently unoccupied: (1) sub-50ms incremental rebuilds at 100k-line scale with full safety checking on; (2) a dependency that never declares network access is statically incapable of it, transitively, including at build time — verified against an adversarial dependency suite; (3) provable data-race freedom with fork/join and SPMD vectorization as IR primitives, ≥90% parallel efficiency to 64 cores, and zero-cost race checking. Ship GPU, distribution and SMT proving as post-1.0 extensions or never.

Two non-negotiable process rules, both learned from other people's expensive mistakes. First: the benchmark harness ships before the claim — public, one command, runnable by a stranger, variance disclosed, hardware pinned. V's unreproducible numbers cost it years of credibility it never fully recovered; the field's two most-cited efficiency studies (Benchmarks Game, Pereira et al.) are both discredited on methodology by their own authors and critics. Publishing a losing number with a method beats publishing a winning number without one. Second: the reference interpreter and the IR fuzzer exist before v0.1, not after. A two-tier backend without differential testing is a miscompilation factory, and for a language whose pitch is safety, one 'safe in debug, wrong in release' bug is worse than a year of delay. Hold a hard complexity budget in the Austral style — a spec that fits in one head — because the highest-probability failure mode of this project is not any technical obstacle listed above. It is attempting all four superlatives at once and stalling pre-1.0, which is precisely what happened to Vale solo and is happening to Carbon with a Google team behind it.

## Corrected claims

- **[compile-speed] Copy-and-patch compilation 'achieves code quality within 10-20% of heavily optimized compilers' and is therefore a near-free way to get optimizing-quality codegen in microseconds.**
  - Why dubious: The primary source says the opposite. arXiv:2011.13127 / OOPSLA'21 reports code that 'runs an order of magnitude faster than interpretation and 14% faster than LLVM -O0', and 39-63% faster than Chrome's Liftoff baseline JIT. The baselines are -O0 and a baseline JIT, not -O2/-O3. The paper does not compare generated code quality against high optimization levels at all. The '10-20%' figure in the survey is fabricated or a garbled reading of 'two orders of magnitude faster compilation than -O0, three orders vs higher levels' (a compile-speed number, not a code-quality number).
  - Corrected: Copy-and-patch is a baseline-tier codegen technique: compile speed ~100x faster than LLVM -O0, code quality roughly -O0-plus-a-bit, i.e. typically 2-5x slower than -O2 on compute-bound code. Adopt it for the dev/debug tier and for comptime evaluation. It cannot serve the release tier, and no 'fastest compiler + best codegen from one backend' story can be built on it. Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.13127
- **[compile-speed] 'mold links ~4.9x faster than lld and 1.9x faster than Wild at the median (Aug 2026 benchmarks)', cited to David Lattimore's 2026-09-18 post and arXiv:2608.23228.**
  - Why dubious: Lattimore's 2026-09-18 post (https://davidlattimore.github.io/posts/2026/09/18/benchmarking-wild-vs-mold.html) reports Wild as roughly comparable to mold, not 1.9x slower: blender-debug and godot-debug ~1.2x slower, blender-release ~0.9x (Wild faster), clang-release equivalent. It explicitly flags confounds (16-core Ryzen vs 64-core Threadripper/M1 Ultra, tmpfs vs disk, whether output files are deleted between runs, mold 2.42.x improvements landing after Wild's August numbers) and does not discuss lld. The cited arXiv ID also does not correspond to a linker benchmarking paper.
  - Corrected: mold and Wild are within noise of each other once hardware and filesystem are controlled; both are several times faster than lld, whose symbol-resolution pass is still sequential. The real, defensible lesson stands (design the object format for fully parallel symbol resolution, and link incrementally), but do not quote a mold-vs-Wild ratio, and never quote cross-hardware linker ratios without pinning filesystem and core count.
- **[parallelism] Zig's std.Io delivers io_uring-class efficiency today: 'a Zig application using coroutines executed just 33 syscalls with io_uring, compared to 677 when falling back to a poll-based system.'**
  - Why dubious: Zig 0.16.0 shipped 2026-04-14 with the colorless std.Io *interface*, but the only usable implementation shipped is std.Io.Threaded (thread pool). std.Io.Evented (io_uring on Linux, kqueue on BSD/macOS) is in-tree but incomplete and, per multiple 0.16 write-ups, does not currently compile. The syscall-count figure is from a prototype/demo, not from shipped 0.16 behaviour.
  - Corrected: The design pattern is validated and worth copying (pass the I/O implementation as an explicit parameter; do not colour functions). The performance claim is unproven in a released toolchain. Treat 'colorless async at io_uring efficiency' as an open engineering problem we would be solving ourselves, not a solved pattern we can inherit. Sources: https://ziglang.org/download/0.16.0/release-notes.html, https://lalinsky.com/2026/05/11/async-io-in-zig-016-today.html
- **[parallelism] Tile-level GPU DSLs are essentially at parity with hand-tuned kernels: 'ML-Triton achieves 94-96% of expert hand-tuned XeTLA' and 'within a 5% margin' for FlashAttention-2-class kernels.**
  - Why dubious: Cherry-picked hardware and baseline. Those numbers are Intel PVC vs XeTLA. On current NVIDIA hardware the gap is large and growing: FlashAttention-4 written in CuTe DSL is reported ~2.7x faster than a Triton implementation of the same kernel on B200, and Triton sustains only 62-101% of cuBLAS on GEMM across platforms. Triton lacks or only experimentally supports the features that define peak on new silicon (warp specialization, TMA, 5th-gen tensor-core instructions), which is precisely why production engines still ship CUTLASS/CuTe.
  - Corrected: A vendor-neutral tile abstraction buys portability and 60-100% of peak, and reliably trails vendor DSLs by 1.5-3x on the newest accelerator features for 1-2 hardware generations. 'Fastest parallelization of any language' on GPUs is therefore structurally unwinnable for anyone who does not ship the silicon. Reprice this goal as portability-per-unit-effort, not peak. Sources: https://arxiv.org/html/2604.23466v2, https://www.spheron.network/blog/cutlass-cute-dsl-writing-gpu-kernels-in-python-2026/
- **[runtime-efficiency] Vale's generational references give memory safety at a measured 2-10.84% overhead and are a live alternative to borrow checking.**
  - Why dubious: Per the Vale author's own 2026 posts, the project stalled mid-roadmap and is being rebooted as 'Valen'; generational references are currently disabled, with revival hoped for, and the stated plan is to land Rust interop and basic safety first and only later attempt 'blending group borrowing with generational references'. The 2-10.84% figure is from an unreleased prototype on the author's own microbenchmarks, never independently reproduced.
  - Corrected: Generational references are an interesting unproven research direction with no shipping implementation and no third-party measurement. Do not put them on the critical path. If used at all, use them behind a fallback design. Source: https://verdagon.dev/blog/golden-spike-reviving-vale-valen
- **[runtime-efficiency] Hylo's mutable value semantics (let/inout/sink/set) give C++/Rust-like performance without a borrow checker — and the finding is sourced to docs.carbon-lang.dev's generics witness-table appendix.**
  - Why dubious: The cited source is a Carbon document about witness tables and says nothing about Hylo or MVS; the survey self-flagged this as unverified yet still promoted it to a high-confidence design lever. Separately, Hylo has no stable release and development has moved to a ground-up rewrite (hylo-lang/hylo-new), so there is no mature implementation whose performance could have been measured.
  - Corrected: The *mechanism* (parameter-passing conventions instead of lifetime inference) is sound, well-specified and cheap to implement — that is the real reason to adopt it. But there is zero performance evidence behind it; treat any 'MVS matches Rust performance' statement as a hypothesis we would have to establish ourselves. Sources: https://hylo-lang.org/introduction/, https://github.com/hylo-lang/hylo
- **[security] Borrow/alias checking is effectively a compile-time non-issue, and safety is 'a solvable engineering problem, not an inherent safety-vs-speed tradeoff'.**
  - Why dubious: Two measured counterpoints. (1) Polonius Alpha, enabled on nightly 2026-08-06, shows worst-case 2-3x compile-time regressions on borrow-heavy crates across the top 10k crates.io sample — flow-sensitive alias checking is expensive. (2) rustc profiles put MIR borrowck around 29% of compile time on some crates. Alias analysis is a first-order compile-speed cost, which collides directly with headline goal 3.
  - Corrected: Runtime safety cost can be driven near zero; compile-time safety-checking cost cannot. This is the strongest argument for choosing a checker with no region/lifetime inference (parameter conventions, or module-local affine ownership) rather than a Rust-class flow-sensitive borrow checker. Source: https://blog.rust-lang.org/2026/08/04/enabling-polonius-alpha-on-nightly/
- **[compile-speed] Zig's self-hosted x86_64 backend cut hello-world from 22.8s to 275ms, 'a ~99% reduction' also described as 'a ~70% class improvement'.**
  - Why dubious: Internally inconsistent (22.8s to 275ms is 98.8%, not 70%), and hello-world is not a compile-speed benchmark — it measures LLVM/libc/link startup on a trivial program, so it overstates the win by an order of magnitude. Cited to a single aggregator article rather than a primary measurement.
  - Corrected: The defensible primary numbers: self-hosted x86_64 is default in Debug since 0.15.1 and makes debug compilation roughly 5x faster; the Zig compiler's own build went 75s -> 20s; real projects report 5-50% wall-clock improvement. Use the self-build and real-project figures as our baselines, never hello-world. Sources: https://mitchellh.com/writing/zig-builds-getting-faster, https://ziglang.org/devlog/2025/
- **[compile-speed] Jai compiles 80-100k LoC/sec (target 1M LoC/sec) and V does 1.5M LoC/sec/core — used as baselines to beat.**
  - Why dubious: Both are self-published demo numbers with no third-party reproduction; one survey correctly flags them and then the other still lists them as reference points. Worse, LoC/sec is not a meaningful metric: it is dominated by header expansion, semantic density, whether optimization runs, and whether linking is included. TCC's famous 859k LoC/s is also stale (TinyCC 0.9.22 vs gcc 3.2, ~2005 hardware) and measures post-preprocessing line counts inflated ~25x by #include.
  - Corrected: Discard LoC/sec entirely. Define compile speed as wall-clock on a pinned corpus + pinned hardware, reported as cold-full, warm-full, and single-token-edit incremental, with linking included. This is also the only way our own claims stay credible — and it is the mistake that permanently damaged V's reputation.
- **[runtime-efficiency] Mojo achieves performance 'competitive with hand-written CUDA/HIP' and is the vendor-neutral portable option.**
  - Why dubious: The same SC'25 paper (arXiv:2509.21039) reports real gaps: AMD atomics, and fast-math compute-bound kernels on both NVIDIA and AMD. Also, the governance framing is now stale in a way that matters: Modular's acquisition by Qualcomm closed 2026-07-28, Mojo 1.0 shipped 2026-08-11, and the compiler was open-sourced under Apache-2.0-with-LLVM-exceptions on 2026-08-18 at ModCon. 'Vendor-neutral' now means 'stewarded by a chip vendor'.
  - Corrected: Mojo is competitive on memory-bound kernels, behind on compute-bound and atomic-heavy ones, and is now an open-source project owned by Qualcomm. Two consequences: (a) it is a credible base to fork/build on rather than only a competitor, and (b) its neutrality is a strategic opening for us, not a moat for them. Sources: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.21039, https://www.modular.com/blog/mojo-open-source, https://www.phoronix.com/news/Modular-Mojo-Open-Source
- **[security] CISA/NSA/FBI 'moved from guidance to a formal expectation', with software manufacturers 'pushed to publish a memory-safety roadmap by January 1, 2026'.**
  - Why dubious: The date and content are right but the force is overstated. The Secure-by-Design material and 'Product Security Bad Practices' are voluntary guidance with no enforcement mechanism for the general market; the language is 'urged'/'encouraged', with a carve-out for products whose support ends before 2030. It shapes federal and OT procurement conversations; it is not regulation.
  - Corrected: Treat this as procurement-driven demand signal, not compliance-driven demand. Useful for positioning (ship unsafe-code inventories and machine-checkable safety evidence as a first-class artifact), but do not build the adoption thesis on a mandate that does not exist. Source: https://www.cisa.gov/case-memory-safe-roadmaps
- **[compile-speed] rustc_codegen_cranelift's modest ~5% total speedup proves 'the biggest wins come from designing the IR and pipeline for speed from the start'.**
  - Why dubious: The inference is wrong even though the number is right. A 20% codegen-time cut producing only ~5% end-to-end means codegen was never the dominant cost in rustc — the frontend (macro expansion, trait solving, borrowck, monomorphization collection) and linking are. The lesson drawn ('design the IR for speed') is not what this data shows.
  - Corrected: The data says: profile before choosing a backend strategy, because in a Rust-class language the fast-backend lever is worth ~5% while the frontend and linker levers are worth multiples. For us that reorders priorities: bounded type inference, shape-based generics, no textual include, acyclic module graph, and incremental linking outrank the choice of dev-tier codegen.
- **[bootstrapping-strategy] Carbon's toolchain architecture and generics design are validated references for a production-grade fast compiler.**
  - Why dubious: Carbon is still explicitly experimental as of 2026: the 0.1 MVP is late-2026 at the earliest and 1.0 is post-2028, with generics, class types and operator overloading still landing in nightlies. Its published memory-safety approach is a design plan, not an implementation.
  - Corrected: Carbon's flat, index-referenced, data-oriented semantic IR and its no-textual-include module model are worth copying as engineering practice (they are well documented and internally consistent). Its generics/witness-table performance and its safety story have no measured outcomes and must not be cited as evidence. Source: https://docs.carbon-lang.dev/docs/project/roadmap.html

## Goal conflicts

- **Goal 1 (runtime efficiency, via monomorphization + heavy comptime metaprogramming) vs Goal 3 (fastest compiler)** — Monomorphization is whole-program, superlinear in nested-generic code, and blows up binary size and i-cache; comptime evaluation on a naive interpreter becomes the dominant compile cost the moment users lean on it (Zig's comptime is reportedly slower than CPython). Both are the classic mechanisms for zero-cost abstraction, and both are exactly what make C++/Rust builds slow.
  - Options: Monomorphize by default, accept slow builds (Rust/C++ path).; Shape-based/dictionary lowering by default with an explicit @specialize attribute for hot paths (Go GC-shape stencil path).; Monomorphize only within a module, dictionary-pass across module boundaries, so cross-module builds stay separately compilable and parallel.; Cache monomorphized instantiations content-addressed across builds so the cost is paid once per (generic, type-args) pair globally.
  - Recommended: Shape-based lowering as the default + explicit opt-in specialization + content-addressed instantiation cache. Rationale: compile speed is the only one of the four goals that is actually winnable, so it wins ties. Specialization is a per-call-site decision we can hand to PGO later; a slow compiler is architectural and unfixable later. Additionally, build the comptime evaluator as a bytecode VM with an inline cache from day one and impose a declared step/alloc budget per comptime evaluation, so metaprogramming cannot silently become an unbounded compile-time hazard.
- **Goal 3 (fastest compiler) vs Goal 4 (fastest parallelism, via MLIR-style multi-level IR + autoscheduling/autotuning)** — Everything that makes Halide/TVM/Triton-class parallel code fast is search: autoschedulers, polyhedral ILP, equality saturation, autotuning. Halide's autoscheduler only beats experts in its 'autotuning' configuration; one-shot is 1.07x geomean. Autotuning is minutes to hours per kernel. A compiler that autotunes is not a fast compiler, and an MLIR dependency also imports a large pass-manager/verifier/build-time footprint.
  - Options: Single pipeline with search on by default: best kernels, unusable dev loop.; Three-tier build: dev (no search), release (one-shot cost-model scheduling), tuned (offline autotuning writing a checked-in schedule/profile artifact).; Keep search entirely out of the compiler; ship it as a separate offline tool producing a committed schedule file the compiler consumes deterministically.; Restrict search to a tiny, structured subdomain (tensor/affine ops only) where the space is small enough for one-shot cost models.
  - Recommended: Three-tier, with the tuned tier's output as a checked-in, human-diffable schedule artifact (option 2+3 combined). Autotuning results must be *data*, not a compiler mode — that keeps compile speed honest, makes builds reproducible, and lets the tuned schedule be reviewed and version-controlled. Do not take an MLIR dependency for v1 unless in-language GPU is in v1 scope; the compile-time and maintenance cost is real and, notably, unmeasured in all five surveys.
- **Goal 3 (fastest compiler, via a fast dev backend) vs Goal 2 (security built in) and the whole notion of a language specification** — A two-tier backend means two independent implementations of the language's semantics. Zig has repeatedly shipped divergences between its self-hosted and LLVM backends (pointer provenance, inline asm, signed-integer representation). For a language whose selling point is safety, 'your program is safe in debug and miscompiles in release' destroys the entire value proposition — and any UB in the spec becomes a place where the two tiers are permitted to disagree.
  - Options: One backend only: give up either dev-loop speed or release codegen quality.; Two backends + a precisely specified, UB-free safe subset + mandatory differential testing (reference interpreter, random program generator, both tiers) in CI from commit one.; Two backends where the fast tier is generated from the slow tier's semantics (e.g. copy-and-patch stencils compiled from the same source of truth), so divergence is structurally hard.; Fast tier is an interpreter/JIT of the same IR the optimizing tier consumes, not a separate codegen.
  - Recommended: Option 2 plus as much of option 3 as possible: zero UB in the safe subset (trap-on-overflow in all modes, defined pointer provenance, defined integer representations, no fast-math by default), copy-and-patch stencils mechanically derived from the same semantic definitions, and a Miri-class reference interpreter plus a Rustlantis-class IR fuzzer running differentially across all three execution engines in CI before v0.1. This is non-negotiable overhead for a security-positioned language.
- **Goal 1 (peak runtime efficiency, via aliasing guarantees and vectorization) vs Goal 2 (default-on safety checks)** — Bounds/overflow checks are nearly free in cycles (~6% on real packet processing, ~0.2-0.3% mispredict) but can silently block auto-vectorization, which is worth 4-16x on the kernels that define 'most efficient language'. Meanwhile the aliasing facts needed to vectorize are exactly the facts LLVM has spent a decade dropping or miscompiling for Rust.
  - Options: Safety off in release: abandons goal 2.; Unsafe escape hatches for hot loops: pushes the cost onto users and reintroduces the CVE classes.; Make in-bounds-ness provable by construction: iterator/slice-based idioms and dependent-ish length types that carry the bound in the type, so the check has nothing to prove at the loop level.; Explicit SPMD/vector execution mode (ISPC-style) with guaranteed vectorization and masked control flow, so vector width is a compile-time contract rather than an optimizer heuristic.
  - Recommended: 3 + 4 together, and encode aliasing as a structural property of our own IR (region/ownership-typed memory) rather than droppable metadata. Provide zero unsafe escape hatch for bounds; provide a checked 'trusted-length' construction whose obligation is discharged once, at slice creation. Explicitly do NOT rely on auto-vectorization for the performance story — auto-vectorization is brittle under divergent control flow and reverts silently to scalar code, which makes it a terrible foundation for a superlative claim.
- **Goal 2 (capability-based security, no ambient authority) vs Goal 1 (efficiency) and basic ergonomics/FFI** — Threading capability values through every signature is viral: it colours functions as surely as async does, bloats signatures, obstructs generic code, and adds a parameter to every leaf call unless the optimizer erases it. Worse, FFI and subprocess are the escape valves that void the whole system — Deno's --allow-run/--allow-ffi demonstrate that one coarse grant reopens ambient authority.
  - Options: Full Austral-style capability values everywhere: maximum assurance, maximum friction.; Module-level declared capability requirements checked at the module graph, with capability values only at the boundary where authority is actually narrowed.; Runtime/OS sandbox only (WASI-style component boundary), no type-level capabilities.; Effect system: capabilities as inferred effects on function types, so signatures stay clean but the graph is still statically checked.
  - Recommended: Option 2 as the v1 mechanism, designed so option 4 can be layered on later. Modules declare the authority they need (fs, net, exec, ffi, clock, rng, env); the module graph is checked at build time and again at link; a dependency that never declares `net` is statically incapable of it, transitively. Capability *values* appear only where authority is narrowed (a `Dir` rooted at a path, a `Subprocess` bound to an argv allowlist). Critically: FFI is itself a declared capability, and a module with `ffi` is marked as voiding its subtree's guarantees in the build report — make the hole visible and auditable rather than pretending it isn't there.
- **Goal 4 (fastest parallelization, via automatic/implicit parallelism) vs Goal 1 (most efficient language)** — Interaction-net evaluation (Bend/HVM2) gives near-ideal scaling to tens of thousands of cores with no annotations, and pays for it with 10-50x worse single-thread performance than optimized C — with community reports far worse (an 11x11 matrix job: ~2 hours single-threaded in Bend vs 22 seconds single-core in Go). Automatic parallelism and peak sequential efficiency are not merely in tension; as default execution models they are mutually exclusive.
  - Options: Interaction nets as the default model: wins goal 4's scaling story, loses goal 1 outright.; Conventional model with explicit fork/join as the default; no implicit parallelism.; Hybrid: compiler detects interaction-net-friendly (pure, non-strict, graph-reduction-shaped) subgraphs and dispatches only those to an alternative runtime.; Conventional model + a pure/effect-free sublanguage that is *permitted* to be auto-parallelized, with the strategy chosen per-region by the programmer or PGO.
  - Recommended: Option 2 as the default with option 4 as the extension. Fork/join spawn/sync as first-class IR instructions (Tapir/OpenCilk-style) so inlining, vectorization and DCE see through parallel regions — this is the actual technical basis for a 'fast compiler for parallel code' claim. Implicit parallelism is a research bet; keep it out of the critical path, and do not let it near the sequential efficiency claim.
- **Goal 2 (formal verification / SMT-backed contracts) vs Goal 3 (fastest compiler)** — An SMT solver in the default build path makes compile time unpredictable and unbounded in exactly the way Swift's constraint solver is (42s to fail on 12 lines) — the pathology our own type-inference rule is meant to forbid. You cannot both forbid unbounded search in type checking and permit it in verification.
  - Options: Contracts runtime-checked only; no prover.; Contracts + opt-in per-module SMT proving, never in the default build, with proofs cached as content-addressed artifacts.; Verification as a separate tool/CI gate outside the compiler entirely.; Prover in default build with a hard per-obligation timeout, falling back to a runtime check.
  - Recommended: Option 2, with option 4's fallback semantics: contract syntax (pre/post/invariant) in the language from day one, runtime-checked by default, statically dischargeable per-module behind an explicit flag, with proof results cached content-addressed and a mandatory timeout that degrades to a runtime check rather than failing the build. Follow Verus rather than Dafny: verify against real machine types including overflow and bit-width, or the proofs won't cover the bugs a systems language actually has.
- **Goal 1 (peak codegen, via LLVM/PGO/LTO/BOLT) vs Goal 2 (reproducible, auditable builds) and Goal 3 (fast builds)** — The 'free performance' pipeline (PGO + LTO + BOLT, up to ~20% on top of FDO+LTO for compiler self-builds) requires profile collection, whole-program LTO, and post-link binary rewriting. LTO is the antithesis of fast and incremental; profiles are inputs that make builds non-reproducible unless versioned; post-link rewriting means the shipped binary is not the linker's output, which complicates both signing and any 'what got compiled is derivable from source' claim that the xz lesson demands.
  - Options: Skip PGO/LTO/BOLT: leave ~20% on the table.; Release-only pipeline, with profiles as checked-in, versioned, content-addressed build inputs.; Sampling-based profiles collected in CI on a fixed workload set, regenerated on a schedule, diffable.; Static profile inference / cost models only, no measured profiles.
  - Recommended: Option 2 + 3: profiles are first-class, versioned, content-addressed build inputs — never ambient files — so a release build remains bit-reproducible given (source, profile, toolchain). Keep PGO/LTO/BOLT strictly out of dev and test tiers. Design the object/executable format for post-link rewriting and in-place patching from the start, and note that Zig's in-place-patching wins are ELF-only: Mach-O code signing and PE/COFF will each need their own answer.
- **Goal 2 (sandboxed, non-Turing-complete build system — the xz lesson) vs Goal 1/3 (comptime metaprogramming power)** — The xz backdoor lived only in build output, not reviewed source. The stated mitigation is a build system with no unsandboxed Turing-complete stage. But our efficiency story depends on powerful compile-time execution, which is exactly an unsandboxed Turing-complete stage that runs on every build with the compiler's authority.
  - Options: Ban compile-time execution: gives up the main efficiency lever.; Declarative build DSL + Turing-complete comptime that is capability-sandboxed (no fs/net/exec unless declared and granted) and deterministic (no clock, no rng, no env).; Turing-complete build scripts sandboxed in WASM.; Trust build scripts as today (Rust build.rs / proc-macro model).
  - Recommended: Option 2. Comptime is pure and deterministic by construction: no ambient I/O, no clock, no environment, no randomness, step and allocation budgets, and every comptime-read file declared as an explicit build input so it lands in the reproducibility hash. The build graph itself is declarative data, not a program. This simultaneously closes the xz-class vector, makes builds reproducible, and keeps comptime fast and cacheable — one decision serving three goals, which is rare here and worth taking.

## Measurable targets

- **1. Most runtime-efficient language in existence**
  - Metric: Geometric-mean wall-clock over a fixed, publicly published benchmark suite, with per-benchmark numbers, ≥10 runs, reported median and p95, pinned hardware and pinned compiler versions; plus source-line and binary-size disclosure per implementation.
  - Benchmark: Tier A: SPEC CPU 2017 intspeed+fpspeed (licensed) or, if unavailable, LLVM test-suite SingleSource/MultiSource + PolyBench/C 4.2 + Rodinia CPU. Tier B: five real applications ported idiomatically (a JSON/protocol parser, an LSM-tree KV store, a ray tracer, an HTTP server, a compression codec). Explicitly NOT the Computer Language Benchmarks Game — its own maintainers state the programs are 'fleetingly small' and some merely test whether the stdlib wraps a C routine.
  - Baseline: best-of(clang -O3 -march=native, gcc -O3 -march=native, rustc -C opt-level=3 -C target-cpu=native) per benchmark, with the C/C++/Rust implementations expert-reviewed rather than naively translated (the exact confound that discredits the Pereira et al. energy rankings).
  - Realism: Parity within 5% geomean is achievable in 3-5 years with an LLVM release backend. Beating best-of-C/Rust by a meaningful margin on general code is not achievable: on well-optimized C the remaining headroom is a few percent, and it is concentrated in aliasing-enabled vectorization and data layout. Honest framing: 'matches C on scalar code and beats it on layout- and vectorization-sensitive code, where SoA types, guaranteed-SPMD vector semantics and structural aliasing give a 1.3-4x win on named kernels.' Targeting a specific, narrow class of wins is defensible; 'most efficient in existence' is not a falsifiable claim and should be retired.
- **2. Security built in**
  - Metric: Four separate falsifiable measures. (a) Safety: fraction of a known-vulnerable C corpus whose memory-safety bugs become deterministic traps rather than exploitable UB when ported. (b) Confinement: fraction of an adversarial dependency suite that can perform undeclared I/O (target: 0). (c) Escape-hatch surface: lines of unsafe/FFI-marked code in compiler + stdlib, published per release, trending down. (d) Safety runtime cost: geomean delta of the Tier A/B suite with checks enabled vs a checks-disabled build.
  - Benchmark: (a) Juliet C/C++ test suite CWE-119/121/122/124/126/127/415/416 subsets plus Magma; (b) a purpose-built adversarial package suite of ~50 dependencies attempting network exfiltration, fs reads outside declared roots, subprocess spawn, and build-time payload injection (an xz-shaped test: payload present only in build output); (c) a published unsafe-code inventory; (d) the Tier A/B suite above.
  - Baseline: (a) C compiled with ASan+UBSan, which misses temporal bugs ASan cannot see; (b) Rust+cargo with cargo-vet/deny, which cannot statically prevent a build.rs or a dependency from opening a socket; (c) rustc+std unsafe-block count as an order-of-magnitude reference; (d) ≤2% geomean, ≤5% worst-case single benchmark.
  - Realism: (a) and (b) are achievable and are the strongest honest claims in the whole project — (b) in particular is something no mainstream toolchain does today, and it is cheap to implement relative to its value. (d) ≤2% geomean is plausible only if bounds checks are eliminated structurally rather than by optimizer heroics; budget for 3-6% initially. Do not promise absence of side channels: source-level constant-time discipline is defeated by Spectre-class transient execution, and compiler-level speculative taint tracking (Serberus-class) is still research, not a settled technique.
- **3. Compiler faster than any current language's compiler**
  - Metric: Wall-clock, linking included, on pinned hardware with a pinned corpus, reported as: cold full build (empty cache), warm full build, single-token-edit incremental (p50 and p95), and full-build throughput normalized per core. Plus a comptime-evaluation throughput number, because comptime becomes the bottleneck the moment it is used. Never LoC/sec.
  - Benchmark: A published, versioned corpus: (i) the self-hosted compiler itself (~150-300k lines), (ii) a synthetic 100k-line/1000-module project with a realistic dependency DAG, (iii) a 10k-line single-module project, (iv) a generics-heavy stress module, (v) a comptime-heavy stress module. All in-repo, one command, results published per commit.
  - Baseline: Go's gc toolchain (the standing champion for a real optimizing-enough compiler at scale) and Zig's self-hosted x86_64 debug backend. Concrete targets: cold debug build of corpus (ii) ≥3x faster than equivalent-size Go; single-token-edit incremental p95 <50ms including link (Zig demonstrates ~30ms in-place ELF patching, so this is a known-reachable bar); comptime evaluator ≥10x CPython on a fixed comptime benchmark (Zig's comptime is reportedly ~20x slower than CPython — that is the trap to avoid); release build ≤4x the debug build's time.
  - Realism: This is the one superlative that is genuinely winnable, and it should be the project's headline. It is an engineering/architecture outcome, not a research outcome: no textual include, mandatory acyclic module graph, imports-first declarations, flat SoA index-referenced IR, per-declaration content-hash query DAG, bounded local type inference, shape-based generics, a copy-and-patch dev tier, and an incrementally patchable object format. Caveat: 'fastest compiler' will be true for debug/incremental builds and false for optimized release builds as long as LLVM is the release backend — say so explicitly rather than letting the claim blur.
- **4. Fastest compute-parallelization performance of any language**
  - Metric: Three separate metrics, because one number here is always a lie. (a) Parallel efficiency: speedup/cores on a fixed kernel set at 8/16/32/64/128 cores. (b) Absolute kernel performance vs vendor-tuned libraries, as % of achieved peak (FLOPs or bandwidth) with roofline position stated. (c) Portability cost: the same source's % of peak on ≥3 targets (x86 AVX-512, ARM SVE/Neon, one GPU), which is the metric a single-source language can actually win.
  - Benchmark: CPU: Parallel Research Kernels + NAS Parallel Benchmarks class + a parallel graph workload (GAP BFS/PageRank) for irregular access. GPU (if in scope): GEMM, FlashAttention-class attention, 3D stencil, SpMV, scan/sort, BabelStream. Never HPL/HPCG — the June 2026 #1, LineShine at 2.198 EF/s on 13.8M cores, wins through custom LX2 silicon, the LingQi interconnect and OS co-design, not language choice; a language cannot beat that on hardware it does not control.
  - Baseline: CPU: clang/gcc + OpenMP, Rust+Rayon, OpenCilk, Kokkos. Target ≥90% parallel efficiency to 64 cores on the regular kernels and ≥ Rayon on all of them (Cilk-style spawn/sync has been shown to match or beat Rayon to 128 cores, so this bar is reachable). GPU: cuBLAS/CUTLASS/CuTe and hipBLASLt. Realistic GPU target is 'within 15% on memory-bound kernels, within 40% on compute-bound', NOT parity.
  - Realism: (a) and (c) are achievable and defensible. (b) is not: production peak on current accelerators requires warp specialization, TMA and newest-generation tensor-core instructions, and the measured state of the art is that even Triton trails CuTe by ~2.7x on FlashAttention-4-class kernels on B200 and sustains 62-101% of cuBLAS on GEMM. Recommendation: drop GPU from v1 entirely, ship structured fork/join + guaranteed SPMD vectorization on CPU as first-class IR primitives, and restate goal 4 as 'highest parallel efficiency and lowest parallel-abstraction overhead on CPU, with provable data-race freedom' — which is measurable, winnable, and not currently claimed by anyone.
- **Cross-cutting: credibility of any claim we publish**
  - Metric: Reproducibility: a third party on specified hardware reproduces published numbers within ±5%; and bit-identical rebuilds of the toolchain from source on 3 independent hosts.
  - Benchmark: An in-repo benchmark harness (one command, pins toolchain versions, records hardware/kernel/governor/turbo state, emits variance) plus a reproducible-build check in CI.
  - Baseline: The failure cases: V's unreproducible LoC/sec claims (lasting 'vaporware' reputation), and the two most-cited efficiency studies in the field — the Benchmarks Game (maintainers' own caveat: programs are tiny and unrepresentative) and Pereira et al. (C++/Ada results diverge from theory, indicating per-language implementation-quality confounds).
  - Realism: Fully achievable and cheap, and it is the single highest-leverage credibility investment available. Hard rule: no comparative performance claim leaves the repo before the harness that produces it is public and runnable by a stranger. Publishing a losing number with a method beats publishing a winning number without one.

## Gaps

- Platform ABI implementation cost is entirely absent from all five surveys. Even with LLVM doing codegen, per-target calling conventions, struct-passing rules, varargs, bitfields, alignment and C-ABI edge cases must be hand-implemented per triple. This is a large, unglamorous, unavoidable cost and a common reason hobby languages stall at 'works on x86-64 Linux'.
- Debug information, debugger integration, profiler integration and sanitizers are never mentioned. DWARF/CodeView emission plus working breakpoints, stack unwinding and variable inspection determine whether anyone can actually use the language, and they interact badly with a copy-and-patch dev tier and with in-place binary patching.
- Windows and macOS are effectively unaddressed. Zig's incremental/in-place-linking wins are ELF-specific; Mach-O code signing invalidates patched binaries and PE/COFF has a different incremental story. The 'millisecond incremental builds' claim is currently a Linux claim.
- Error-handling model, unwinding, and the ABI of failure propagation are never discussed, despite direct consequences for codegen quality, binary size, FFI, and whether the safe subset can be UB-free.
- Numeric and floating-point semantics are missing: fast-math, reassociation, FMA contraction, and determinism vs vectorization. This collides simultaneously with goal 1 (vectorization needs reassociation), reproducibility, and verification (proofs need defined FP semantics).
- Parallel determinism is conflated with data-race freedom throughout. Race freedom does not give reproducible results for floating-point reductions or work-stealing schedules. Whether the language promises deterministic parallel results is an unmade decision with large performance consequences.
- SIMD/portable-vector type design is unaddressed despite vectorization being the core efficiency lever: fixed-width vector types vs scalable vectors (SVE/RVV), how vector width enters the type system, and how SPMD masking is specified.
- MLIR's own compile-time and maintenance cost is entirely unmeasured, yet three of five surveys recommend adopting it. Nobody quantified pass-manager/verifier overhead, build footprint, or the cost of tracking upstream MLIR churn — load-bearing for the collision between goals 3 and 4.
- Memory allocator design and the syscall/IO abstraction layer are absent, despite 'explicit allocators, no hidden allocation' being a headline recommendation. Allocator quality often dominates real-application performance more than codegen does.
- Package manager semantics beyond security are unaddressed: version resolution, semver policy, and specifically how capability metadata interacts with version updates (a dependency that silently gains a `net` requirement in a patch release).
- Standard library scope and its own safety/performance budget are never scoped. This is typically comparable in effort to the compiler and is where most 'efficiency' claims are actually won or lost.
- No adoption thesis. Who switches, from what, and why — beyond 'C header import'. Migration from C++ and Rust, and whether the language's niche is one where build times actually dominate developer pain, are unexamined.
- No governance, licensing, funding or maintenance-burden plan; specifically, no estimate for tracking LLVM's release cadence, which is a permanent recurring tax on the release backend.
- Unverified claims left standing that would change decisions if wrong: Zig comptime being ~20x slower than CPython (single issue thread plus secondary reporting); Odin #soa performance impact (documentation, no measurements); BOLT's ~20% gains outside datacenter/compiler-self-build workloads; CHERI hardware availability and cost in 2026 beyond Morello research boards; Chapel's 'on par with C/C++' claim under independent 2025-2026 reproduction.
- No cost model for what codegen quality a solo-built optimizing backend can reach, which is the pivotal unknown for whether goal 1 can ever be pursued without LLVM.
- No treatment of how the capability system survives dynamic linking, plugins, and dlopen — the module-graph check is a static-link-time guarantee, and the dynamic case is where WASI-style boundary enforcement would be needed.

## Survey: runtime-efficiency

Language-level performance in 2026 is dominated by a handful of proven levers: aliasing/provenance guarantees that unlock vectorization, value semantics plus explicit allocators that eliminate hidden costs, compile-time metaprogramming that moves work off the runtime path, and native data-layout control (SoA) that maximizes cache and SIMD efficiency. Zig, Odin, Jai, and Mojo show that baking these into the language (rather than bolting them on as library tricks) gives large, measured wins with minimal syntactic cost. Rust demonstrates that safety and speed are not opposed, but also exposes real friction: LLVM's noalias/provenance model is buggy and incompletely exploited even after a decade of investment, and monomorphization can bloat code and hurt cache/i-cache behavior even as it wins on raw throughput versus Swift-style witness-table dictionary passing. Compiler speed is a separable, first-class design axis: Zig's move to a self-hosted x86 backend for debug builds cut compile times by ~70-90% versus LLVM, proving compiler speed is mostly an engineering/IR choice, not a law of nature. Mojo's adoption of MLIR shows multi-level IR is now the credible path to unifying CPU/GPU codegen and enabling aggressive, domain-specific rewrites that plain LLVM IR structurally cannot express. Newer memory-safety approaches (Vale's generational references, Hylo's mutable value semantics) suggest borrow-checking is not the only fast, safe answer, and may be more teachable/compilable. Post-link and profile-guided optimization (PGO+LTO+BOLT) remain a large, underused source of free performance (up to ~20% on top of PGO+LTO) that a from-scratch language should design its build pipeline around from day one. Finally, credible "fastest language" claims require extreme methodological care: the Benchmarks Game and the Pereira et al. energy study are both widely cited yet explicitly flagged by their own authors and critics as narrow, small-kernel, non-representative measurements — any efficiency claim for a new language must be backed by broader, reproducible workloads (SPEC CPU, PolyBench, real applications) with disclosed variance, not microbenchmark leaderboards.

- **LLVM's noalias/provenance handling for Rust references is still an active source of missed optimizations and miscompilation risk after 10+ years of work; metadata/attributes frequently 'get lost' through optimization passes, and llvm.assume doesn't propagate far enough.** (high)
  - Evidence: Nikita Popov's Oct 2024 LLVM Dev Meeting keynote 'Rust ❤ LLVM' states Rust conveys strong guarantees (noalias, readonly, dereferenceable, nonnull, range) via attributes/metadata/assumes, but 'metadata/attributes get lost and assumes don't get lost enough'; rustc has historically had to disable mut-pointer noalias due to LLVM miscompiles (rust-lang/rust#31681, #53105).
  - Implication: Design our IR so aliasing facts are first-class, structural properties of the type system (not attributes bolted onto a lower-level IR) so they cannot be silently dropped by generic optimization passes — e.g., encode noalias/restrict semantics in the SSA/region representation itself, not as removable metadata.
  - Sources: https://llvm.org/devmtg/2024-10/slides/keynote/Popov-Rust_Heart_LLVM.pdf https://github.com/rust-lang/rust/issues/53105 https://github.com/rust-lang/rust/issues/31681
- **Rust's bounds checks have near-zero direct cost on modern superscalar CPUs (branch predictors handle them essentially free) but can have large indirect cost by blocking auto-vectorization.** (high)
  - Evidence: matklad's bounds-check-cost benchmark and Readyset's writeup show real-world packet processing sees only ~6% more cycles despite added checks, because mispredict rate is ~0.2-0.3%; the real cost shows up when a bounds check prevents the compiler from proving in-bounds access for `for i in 0..n` style vectorizable loops.
  - Implication: Prioritize compiler-level range/loop analysis for bounds-check elimination (proving loop bounds statically) over trying to remove checks via unsafe escape hatches; expose an explicit 'trusted length' or iterator-based indexing idiom that guarantees vectorizable, check-free codegen without unsafe code.
  - Sources: https://github.com/matklad/bounds-check-cost https://readyset.io/blog/bounds-checks https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bounds-checking_elimination
- **Zig's move to a self-hosted x86_64 backend (bypassing LLVM) for Debug builds delivered dramatic compile-time wins: hello-world dropped from 22.8s to 275ms (~99% reduction reported), and the Zig compiler's own self-build dropped from 75s to 20s.** (medium)
  - Evidence: BigGo News coverage of Zig's new x86 backend cites these concrete before/after numbers, framing it as a ~70% class improvement for larger builds and far more dramatic for trivial programs, at the cost of some compiler stability/codegen-quality tradeoffs versus LLVM.
  - Implication: Architect the compiler with a fast, non-LLVM native backend for debug/dev-loop builds and reserve LLVM (or an internal MLIR-style optimizing backend) for release builds — a two-backend strategy is the proven way to be both 'fastest compiler' (dev loop) and highly optimized (release), rather than a single backend trying to do both.
  - Sources: https://biggo.com/news/202506090712_Zig_x86_Backend_Performance_Boost
- **Zig's comptime execution engine, while powerful for eliminating runtime overhead, is itself slow — roughly 20x slower than interpreted Python for compile-time computation — making it a bottleneck for heavy metaprogramming (e.g., JSON parsing at compile time can take minutes).** (medium)
  - Evidence: Community reporting and ziglang/zig issue #4055 (tracking comptime performance vs CPython) document comptime speed complaints; case studies report large runtime wins (40% overhead cut) from moving logic to comptime, but with real compile-time cost tradeoffs for heavy use.
  - Implication: Design compile-time evaluation (our equivalent of comptime/#run/constexpr) with its own optimizing interpreter or JIT from day one, not a naive tree-walking interpreter — comptime speed is a first-order compiler-speed concern once users lean on it heavily, not a niche corner case.
  - Sources: https://github.com/ziglang/zig/issues/4055 https://medium.com/@chopra.kanta.73/zigs-comptime-the-compile-time-execution-that-saved-me-40-runtime-cost-473ce95b7cf6
- **Mojo is built directly on MLIR (multi-level IR) rather than plain LLVM IR, which lets it represent high-level domain semantics (e.g., GPU kernel structure) and progressively lower them, achieving performance competitive with hand-written CUDA/HIP on scientific HPC kernels across NVIDIA Hopper/Ampere and AMD MI300 GPUs.** (medium)
  - Evidence: SC'25 workshop paper 'Mojo: MLIR-Based Performance-Portable HPC Science Kernels on GPUs' reports competitive results on stencil, BabelStream, miniBUDE, and Hartree-Fock benchmarks across H100 and MI300A, crediting MLIR-driven optimizations (e.g., reduced register-memory ops confirmed by profiling) and vendor-agnostic GPU modules for portability.
  - Implication: Adopt a multi-level IR (progressive lowering: high-level domain ops -> mid-level loop/tensor ops -> low-level machine ops) instead of a single flat IR, so the compiler can apply high-level rewrites (fusion, layout choice, tiling) before information needed for those rewrites is lost to lowering — this directly targets the 'vectorization failures' and 'lost aliasing info' pitfalls of C/C++/Rust pipelines.
  - Sources: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.21039 https://quantumzeitgeist.com/computing-performance-mojo-mlir-based-kernels-competitive-h100-amd-mi300a/
- **Odin gives struct-of-arrays (SoA) layout as a single built-in language annotation (`#soa`) with automatic transform support in slices/arrays, versus Zig and most mainstream languages where AoS-to-SoA conversion is a manual, invasive refactor.** (high)
  - Evidence: Odin documentation and community forum threads describe `#soa` as changing layout with one annotation on the struct declaration and preserving normal indexing syntax; contrasted explicitly against Zig, where 'you write the SOA layout manually.'
  - Implication: Make data layout (AoS vs SoA, and arbitrary tiled/blocked layouts) a type-level, annotatable property with automatic indexing-syntax translation, not something the programmer must hand-encode with parallel arrays — this preserves ergonomics while unlocking cache/SIMD-friendly layouts by default.
  - Sources: https://odin-lang.org/docs/overview/ https://forum.odin-lang.org/t/how-soa-works-under-the-hood/383 https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AoS_and_SoA
- **Generic-code specialization strategy is a real, measured three-way tradeoff: monomorphization (Rust/C++/our likely default) gives the best runtime performance but can bloat binaries enough to cause cache misses and paradoxically hurt performance at scale; dictionary/witness-table passing (Swift, Go pre-1.18-style, Java-style erasure) gives smaller code but adds indirect-call/load overhead; hybrid schemes exist to split the difference.** (medium)
  - Evidence: The 'Generic Go to Go: Dictionary-Passing, Monomorphisation, and Hybrid' paper and Tristan Hume's survey both document that monomorphizing translators 'generate programs with better runtime performance' while non-specializing (dictionary) translators produce smaller code, and that excessive monomorphization can cause 'excessive cache misses' and 'very bad runtime performance' at scale; Swift's ABI-stability requirement forces witness tables and measurable protocol-witness-table load overhead.
  - Implication: Default to monomorphization for hot-path/small-scale generics but give the compiler (or the programmer via an attribute) the option to fall back to dictionary-passing for rarely-instantiated or huge generic surfaces, to cap code-size/i-cache blowup — i.e., ship a hybrid strategy rather than picking one extreme dogmatically.
  - Sources: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2208.06810 https://thume.ca/2019/07/14/a-tour-of-metaprogramming-models-for-generics/ https://dl.acm.org/doi/pdf/10.1145/3360590
- **Post-link binary optimization (LLVM BOLT) delivers substantial speedups even after PGO+LTO have already been applied: up to 7% on datacenter workloads on top of PGO+LTO, and up to 20.4% on top of FDO+LTO for compiling GCC/Clang themselves (up to 52.1% without prior FDO/LTO).** (high)
  - Evidence: The original BOLT paper (Panchenko et al., CGO 2019 / arXiv:1807.06735) reports these exact figures for Meta production workloads (TAO 2%, RocksDB db_bench fillseq 2.5% on top of PGO) and compiler self-build benchmarks; CPython's adoption of BOLT shows ~1% on pyperformance and ~4% on Pyston macrobenchmarks.
  - Implication: Design the toolchain/build pipeline to natively support a BOLT-like post-link, profile-guided binary-layout optimization pass as a first-class release mode (not an external afterthought), since it is empirically complementary to (additive with) traditional PGO+LTO rather than redundant with it.
  - Sources: https://arxiv.org/pdf/1807.06735 https://research.facebook.com/publications/bolt-a-practical-binary-optimizer-for-data-centers-and-beyond/
- **Equality saturation via e-graphs (the 'egg' library) has moved from academic technique to production compiler use: Cranelift (the WASM/Rust codegen backend) uses an e-graph-based mid-end IR, though it deliberately implements a lighter-weight variant (dedup-on-insert) rather than full fixed-point equality saturation, trading completeness for compile-time speed.** (high)
  - Evidence: The bytecodealliance RFC 'cranelift-egraph' and the egg CACM research highlight/PLDI paper describe Cranelift as 'among the first to use e-graphs and equality saturation in a production-grade compiler,' explicitly noting it 'does not do full equality saturation' — only recognizes equalities the first time a node is inserted — to avoid the fixed-point loop's cost.
  - Implication: Consider an e-graph-based mid-level IR for our optimizer, but follow Cranelift's pragmatic middle ground (single-pass congruence closure, not full saturation) to get much of equality saturation's rewrite power (finding non-obvious equivalent forms, avoiding rewrite-order sensitivity/phase-ordering problems) without paying full saturation's compile-time cost — directly serves both the 'best codegen' and 'fastest compiler' goals simultaneously.
  - Sources: https://github.com/bytecodealliance/rfcs/blob/main/accepted/cranelift-egraph.md https://cacm.acm.org/research-highlights/egg-fast-and-extensible-equality-saturation/ https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3434304
- **Vale's 'generational references' offer memory safety with zero aliasing cost and no GC pauses and no borrow-checker, explicitly positioned as faster/more-flexible than Rust-style borrow checking for patterns borrow checking handles poorly (observers, back-references, graphs, dependency injection).** (medium)
  - Evidence: vale.dev's own design docs and Verdagon's 'Making C++ Memory-Safe Without Borrow Checking, Reference Counting, or Tracing GC' post describe generational references as giving single ownership without GC/RC/borrow-checking overhead, aimed at patterns that fight the Rust borrow checker.
  - Implication: Treat borrow-checking as one point in a broader design space (documented in Verdagon's 'grimoire' of ~13 memory-safety approaches) rather than the only credible fast-and-safe mechanism; evaluate generational references or region-based schemes (Hylo's mutable value semantics) as alternatives or complements, especially for graph-heavy or GUI-style code where borrow-checking causes friction and workaround costs (Rc<RefCell<>> churn).
  - Sources: https://vale.dev/memory-safe https://verdagon.dev/blog/vale-memory-safe-cpp https://verdagon.dev/grimoire/grimoire
- **Hylo's mutable value semantics (MVS) use parameter-passing conventions (let/inout/sink/set) instead of a borrow checker to give C++/Rust-like performance with value semantics and no lifetime annotations, aiming to be simpler to learn and to reason about aliasing than Rust's borrow checker.** (medium)
  - Evidence: Design docs referenced describe four parameter conventions: immutable 'let' (default, like Rust &), 'inout' for mutation, 'sink' for ownership transfer, and 'set' for callee-side initialization — this is a distinct mechanism from Rust's lifetime-based borrow checker for achieving similar aliasing guarantees.
  - Implication: Consider a parameter-convention-based (rather than lifetime-annotation-based) approach to aliasing/ownership as a way to get most of Rust's noalias-enabling guarantees with a gentler learning curve and simpler compiler implementation (no region inference needed), which also serves the 'fastest compiler' goal by avoiding expensive borrow-check/region-inference passes.
  - Sources: https://docs.carbon-lang.dev/docs/design/generics/appendix-witness.html
- **The influential Pereira et al. 'Energy Efficiency across Programming Languages' study (2017/2021) ranking ~27 languages by energy, time, and memory has drawn methodology criticism: languages like C++ and Ada, expected to show negligible overhead versus C by design, showed unexpected performance gaps in the study's results, suggesting benchmark-implementation artifacts rather than fundamental language limits.** (medium)
  - Evidence: Multiple secondary sources (Hackaday 2024 coverage, arXiv:2410.05460 'It's Not Easy Being Green') and the original greenlab.di.uminho.pt paper are cited; the critique specifically flags that C++/Ada 'should see no severe performance penalty over C due to their design' yet the study's numbers don't reflect that expectation, pointing to benchmark-suite (Debian Computer Language Benchmarks Game derived) artifacts.
  - Implication: Any energy/efficiency claim we make for our language must control for benchmark-implementation quality per language (idiomatic, expert-reviewed code, not naive translations) and report variance/implementation notes, since the most-cited energy study in the field is itself criticized for exactly this confound.
  - Sources: https://hackaday.com/2024/09/10/assessing-the-energy-efficiency-of-programming-languages/ https://arxiv.org/html/2410.05460v1 https://greenlab.di.uminho.pt/wp-content/uploads/2017/09/paperSLE.pdf
- **The Computer Language Benchmarks Game's own maintainers/documentation acknowledge its programs are 'fleetingly small, and behave in ways that are significantly different than real applications,' and that some benchmarks (e.g., reverse-complement) mainly measure whether a language's standard library wraps a fast C routine rather than measuring the language/compiler itself.** (high)
  - Evidence: Wikipedia's summary of the Benchmarks Game methodology and HN discussion both state this self-acknowledged limitation; the benchmarks are widely cited in language-marketing contexts despite this explicit caveat from their own creators.
  - Implication: Do not use Benchmarks-Game-style microbenchmark wins as our primary 'most efficient language' evidence; instead validate against broader, more representative suites (SPEC CPU, PolyBench for numerical/loop-heavy kernels, and ideally real application workloads) and be transparent that no single benchmark suite is sufficient for an 'most efficient language in existence' claim.
  - Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Computer_Language_Benchmarks_Game https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=23234470
- **Chapel demonstrates that first-class language support for locality (PGAS — Partitioned Global Address Space) and data-parallel array operations can reach performance 'on par with C and C++' for HPC workloads, while remaining far higher-level (no manual MPI/message-passing) than conventional HPC languages.** (medium)
  - Evidence: Chapel's own documentation and academic surveys describe Chapel's PGAS model exposing locality as a first-class, queryable/controllable property of data across single-host shared-memory and multi-host distributed-memory execution, with performance claims validated in a 2022 PMAM comparative study against conventional environments and continued 2025 optimization research (e.g., prefetching into Chapel's remote data cache for irregular access).
  - Implication: If targeting best-in-class parallelization, treat locality/data-distribution as a first-class type/language concept (like Chapel's domains and locales) rather than a library (like MPI) bolted on afterward — this is what lets the compiler reason about and optimize communication patterns instead of treating them as opaque function calls.
  - Sources: https://chapel-lang.org/ https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3528425.3529104 https://chapel-lang.org/blog/posts/30years/
- **Rust bounds-check and safety overhead is empirically small in real systems code (network drivers), reinforcing that a safe-by-default language need not sacrifice much throughput versus unsafe C, provided the optimizer can eliminate/predict most checks.** (medium)
  - Evidence: 'The Case for Writing Network Drivers in High-Level Programming Languages' (arXiv:1909.06344) is cited alongside the bounds-check-cost measurements showing modest overhead (~6% cycles) for real packet-processing workloads.
  - Implication: Make safety checks (bounds, null, overflow) the default, not opt-in, and invest optimizer effort into eliminating/predicting them — the data suggests this is a solvable engineering problem, not an inherent safety-vs-speed tradeoff, which directly supports the 'security built-in without sacrificing speed' goal.
  - Sources: https://arxiv.org/pdf/1909.06344 https://readyset.io/blog/bounds-checks
- **Mojo integrates Python interop at runtime with compile-time-specialized, performance-portable GPU code, using the same vendor-agnostic MLIR-based GPU modules across NVIDIA and AMD hardware, described as a distinguishing feature versus CUDA-locked or single-vendor approaches.** (medium)
  - Evidence: Search summary of SC'25 workshop paper and QuantumZeitgeist coverage: 'Reusing the same vendor-agnostic Mojo GPU modules adds a unique characteristic for language-embedded GPU portability,' with AMD MI300 series support announced June 2025 alongside existing NVIDIA Hopper/Ampere support.
  - Implication: Design parallel/GPU compute primitives at the IR level to be hardware-vendor-neutral by construction (lowering to vendor backends only at the final codegen stage via MLIR-style dialects), rather than baking a single vendor's execution model into the language surface — this directly targets 'fastest compute parallelization across' the widest hardware set, not just one vendor.
  - Sources: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.21039 https://quantumzeitgeist.com/computing-performance-mojo-mlir-based-kernels-competitive-h100-amd-mi300a/

Pitfalls:
- Treating Computer Language Benchmarks Game results as evidence of general 'fastest language' claims — the maintainers themselves say the programs are small and behave unlike real applications, and some benchmarks just measure whether a stdlib wraps a fast C routine
- Citing the Pereira et al. energy-efficiency study uncritically — its C++/Ada results diverge from theoretical expectations, suggesting benchmark-implementation-quality confounds rather than true language-level effects
- Assuming LLVM's noalias/attribute system will faithfully preserve aliasing guarantees through arbitrary optimization pipelines — Rust's decade-long experience shows attributes/metadata get silently dropped or cause miscompiles
- Assuming monomorphization is a free lunch — at large generic surface area it can cause binary bloat and net-negative performance from icache/dcache pressure
- Assuming a single IR (flat LLVM-IR-style) can serve both 'fastest compiler' and 'best codegen' goals — real fast compilers (Zig) and best-codegen compilers (Mojo/MLIR) diverge in IR strategy, suggesting a two-tier or multi-level approach is necessary
- Assuming heavy compile-time metaprogramming is free — Zig's comptime engine is reportedly ~20x slower than CPython for compute-heavy compile-time code, so an underpowered comptime interpreter becomes a compiler-speed liability
- Conflating 'safety has no cost' with 'safety checks can always be optimized away' — bounds checks are usually cheap but can silently block vectorization, which is an indirect and easy-to-miss cost during language/optimizer design

## Survey: compile-speed

The fastest compilers achieve their speed through a consistent set of overlapping strategies: language designs that make parsing and semantic analysis embarrassingly parallel (no textual includes, context-free grammars, order-independent top-level declarations), direct machine-code emission that skips heavyweight IR pipelines like LLVM for debug/dev builds, incremental and query-based compilation that only recomputes what changed (rustc's red-green query system, salsa, Carbon's incremental IR, Zig's in-place binary patching), data-oriented compiler internals (struct-of-arrays ASTs/IR as in Zig's ZIR and Carbon's semantic IR) that dramatically cut cache misses during compilation itself, and treating the linker as a first-class, parallelized bottleneck (mold, wild). A recurring tension is the "tiered backend" trade-off: fast non-optimizing backends (Zig's self-hosted x86_64/aarch64, rustc's Cranelift, TCC's direct codegen) buy huge speed at dev-time but risk semantic divergence/bugs versus the optimizing backend used for release, which is a real, documented cost (Zig LLVM-vs-self-hosted miscompilation bugs). Generics/metaprogramming strategy is a first-order lever: monomorphization gives excellent runtime perf but can blow up compile time/binary size exponentially in pathological cases, whereas erasure/shared-shape approaches (Go's GC-shape stenciling) trade a little runtime performance for compile-time and binary-size linearity. Expressive type inference (Swift's global constraint solver) can produce genuinely exponential worst-case compile times from innocuous-looking code, arguing strongly for bounded, local, non-backtracking inference in a new language. Copy-and-patch compilation is a proven technique (now used in CPython, Lua, R baseline JITs) for producing near-optimizing-compiler-quality code in microseconds without any traditional codegen pass, and is a strong candidate as the "instant feedback" backend for a new systems language.

- **Zig's self-hosted x86_64 backend, used by default for Debug builds since 0.15.1, cuts debug build wall-clock time roughly 5x versus routing through LLVM, with individual project measurements showing 5-50% wall-clock improvement.** (high)
  - Evidence: Self-hosted x86 backend default in Debug mode; 5% to 50% wall-clock improvements reported across projects switching to it.
  - Implication: Ship two backends from day one: a dead-simple, non-optimizing direct-to-machine-code backend for edit/debug loops and comptime evaluation, and a separate optimizing backend for release — do not try to make one backend serve both goals.

- **Zig's new self-hosted ELF linker with in-place binary patching achieves ~30ms incremental rebuilds of real projects (including the Zig compiler compiling itself) by patching only the changed function's bytes in the existing executable rather than relinking.** (medium)
  - Evidence: Self-hosted ELF linker demonstrated ~30ms incremental rebuilds, in-place binary patching updates only the changed function's code in the output file (May 2026 status).
  - Implication: Design the object/executable format and linker as a first-class, mutable, incrementally-patchable artifact from the start, not a batch/append-only output — this is what makes millisecond incremental builds possible, not just a faster batch linker.

  - Implication: If we adopt a tiered-backend strategy, define language semantics precisely enough (e.g. explicit UB boundaries, no backend-dependent integer/pointer behavior) that fast and optimizing backends are required to agree bit-for-bit on defined behavior, and build a differential-testing harness between backends into CI from day one.

  - Evidence: Original arxiv/ACM paper: template-JIT-style baseline compiler producing full compilers or bytecode assemblers; adopted in PEP 744 (CPython JIT) and R/Lua baseline JIT implementations; 'compilation speeds measured in microseconds' with code quality 10-20% of optimizing compilers.
  - Implication: Use copy-and-patch as the codegen strategy for our 'fast debug/incremental' compiler tier instead of hand-writing a simplistic instruction selector — it's a well-studied, reusable technique that gets most of an optimizing compiler's code quality at a fraction of the engineering and runtime cost.

  - Evidence: Benchmark: mold 4.9x faster than lld, 1.9x faster than wild at median; lld parallelizes input-file work/relocation scanning/output writing but symbol resolution stays sequential.
  - Implication: The linker is as much a bottleneck as the compiler frontend — design our object format/symbol table to allow fully parallel symbol resolution (e.g. avoid global mutable symbol tables, prefer per-TU interned IDs resolved via lock-free merge) so our own linker (or integration with mold) isn't gated by a sequential resolution pass.

  - Evidence: 2026 Rust Project Goals doc: parallel front-end progressed with rustc-rayon added to the tree and enabled in bootstrap, but incremental-compilation race conditions and query-cycle-error inconsistencies are still being resolved; no robust correctness-testing harness yet.
  - Implication: Parallelizing an incremental query-based compiler correctly (deterministic results, no data races between dependent queries) is a multi-year, genuinely hard engineering problem even for a well-funded team — budget for it explicitly and consider designing the dependency graph to be acyclic and coarse-grained by construction (module-level, not expression-level) to sidestep rustc's cycle-detection pain.

  - Evidence: ~20% codegen-time reduction, ~5% total compile speedup on Zed/Tauri/hickory-dns; distributed nightly, recommended for local dev/CI on Linux/macOS.
  - Implication: A fast alternate backend retrofitted onto a language/IR designed for an optimizing compiler (LLVM) yields only modest gains (~5% total) — confirms that biggest wins come from designing the IR and pipeline for speed from the start, not bolting a fast backend onto an existing slow pipeline later.
  - Sources: https://rust-lang.github.io/rust-project-goals/2025h2/production-ready-cranelift.html https://github.com/rust-lang/rustc_codegen_cranelift
- **Carbon's toolchain is architected around a flat, data-oriented semantic IR (replacing the traditional AST) with contiguous array storage and index-based references instead of heap-allocated nodes, explicitly designed from day one to support incremental computation so the same infrastructure can serve batch builds, IDE language servers, and linters without re-architecture.** (high)
  - Evidence: Pipeline: Source → Lex → Parse → Check → Lower → LLVM IR; tokens stored in contiguous arrays with indices; toolchain priorities ranked correctness > code quality > compilation performance > diagnostics quality; architecture designed for incremental computation from inception.
  - Implication: Choose a flat, array-based, index-referenced IR (no pointer-chasing tree of heap nodes) as the single internal representation used by parser, checker, and lowering from the very first prototype — retrofitting data-oriented layout later (as Zig had to) costs significant rework.

  - Evidence: 20% reduction in wall-clock time for tokenizer/AST creation, 40% reduction in wall-clock time for IR generation, via struct-of-arrays (MultiArrayList) with a near drop-in API change.
  - Implication: Build the compiler's own core data structures (token stream, AST, IR) as struct-of-arrays / SoA from the start — this is a compiler-implementation performance technique independent of language design and should be baked into our own compiler's engineering standards, not treated as an optional later optimization.
  - Sources: https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=32349496
- **Go's compile speed comes from language-design choices that eliminate textual preprocessing and symbol-table-dependent parsing: no header files (only compiled package interfaces are consulted), explicit-only imports declared at the top of the file so the compiler need not scan the whole file to find dependencies, no operator/method overloading or C++-style templates, and a strict no-cycles package dependency graph that enables parallel package compilation.** (high)
  - Evidence: No header files/textual includes; imports listed at top so full-file scanning is unnecessary; disallows circular package dependencies enabling parallel builds; direct compilation to native machine code with no bytecode/VM step.
  - Implication: Enforce an acyclic module/package dependency graph as a language rule (compile error on cycles) and require all imports/dependencies to be declared before any other top-level content, so the compiler can build the whole-program dependency DAG and schedule parallel compilation without a full-file preliminary scan.

- **Jonathan Blow's Jai compiler has publicly demoed compiling roughly 80,000-100,000 lines of code in under a second (and even ~1.5s for a full game build), with a stated long-term target of one million lines per second; achieved via a from-scratch compiler (no LLVM in the fast path), a bytecode interpreter for compile-time execution, and aggressive avoidance of unnecessary work rather than any single silver-bullet trick.** (medium)
  - Evidence: Public demos show ~80,000 line codebase compiling in under a second; Blow states a goal of 100k loc/sec and long-term million-lines-per-second target.
  - Implication: Treat these numbers as marketing/demo claims, not peer-reviewed benchmarks (no independent third-party reproduction found) — set our own compile-speed targets against reproducible, independently-measurable benchmarks (e.g. a public corpus with a documented methodology) rather than citing Jai's numbers as a baseline to beat.

- **TCC (Tiny C Compiler) compiles at roughly 859,000 lines/sec (vs ~98,000 lines/sec for gcc -O0) and compiles a real 77k-line project (1.95M lines after header expansion) in 2.27s vs 20.0s for gcc -O0 — roughly 9-10x faster — primarily because it emits machine code directly without any bytecode/IR intermediate stage.** (high)
  - Evidence: TinyCC 0.9.22: 859,000 LoC/s vs gcc 3.2 -O0's 98,000 LoC/s; Links Browser (76,936 lines, 1,950,947 expanded lines) compiles in 2.27s (TCC) vs 20.0s (gcc -O0); no bytecode IR used.
  - Implication: Direct single-pass-to-machine-code compilation (skipping a generic IR entirely for a 'fast' tier) is a proven, decades-old technique for extreme compile speed — worth offering as an explicit 'tcc-style' minimal codegen mode for quick iteration/scripting use of our language, separate from the optimizing pipeline.
  - Sources: https://bellard.org/tcc/ https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=22200869
- **Monomorphization (used by Rust and C++ templates) gives full specialization and optimal runtime performance but requires whole-program knowledge, can blow up compile time and binary size exponentially in pathological nested-generic cases, and is fundamentally a whole-program (non-separately-compilable) technique; Go's 'GC-shape stenciling' takes a middle path, sharing one compiled body across types with the same GC shape to bound code growth while keeping most performance.** (high)
  - Evidence: Erasure is linear in code size; monomorphization can suffer exponential blowup and requires whole-program compilation; Go's GC-shape stenciling monomorphizes only down to a type's GC shape so same-shape types share a compiled body.
  - Implication: Adopt a Go-style shape-based generics strategy by default (share codegen across type arguments with identical layout/GC-shape, monomorphize/specialize only when the user explicitly opts in for a hot path) — this bounds both compile time and binary size while still allowing full monomorphization as an escape hatch for perf-critical generic code.
  - Sources: https://www.rustfaq.org/en/what-is-monomorphization/ https://rednafi.com/go/gc-shape-stenciling/ https://arxiv.org/pdf/2005.11710

  - Evidence: Documented case: Swift 6 spends 42s on 12 lines on M1 Pro then fails with 'unable to type-check in reasonable time'; root cause is bidirectional type inference plus overloading causing the constraint solver to explore exponentially many disjunctions; team says the current architecture's exponential worst case is 'unavoidable.'
  - Implication: Explicitly reject unrestricted global/bidirectional type inference combined with ad-hoc overloading; require local, syntax-directed (Hindley-Milner-bounded or fully explicit) type inference with no cross-statement backtracking, and disallow or tightly restrict operator/function overload resolution that depends on target-type propagation, so type checking stays provably linear (or low-polynomial) per expression.
  - Sources: https://danielchasehooper.com/posts/why-swift-is-slow/ https://www.cocoawithlove.com/blog/2016/07/12/type-checker-issues.html https://mjtsai.com/blog/2025/11/14/roadmap-for-improving-the-swift-type-checker.html
- **Carbon and Zig both explicitly reject C/C++-style textual '#include' preprocessing in favor of precompiled interface/module data (Carbon: compiled package interfaces; general modern-language pattern) — textual includes are one of the primary reasons C/C++ compile slowly since headers get re-lexed/re-parsed in every translation unit that includes them.** (high)
  - Evidence: Go/Carbon-style module design avoids textual inclusion; TCC/gcc comparison shows a 76,936-line C file expands to 1,950,947 lines after header inclusion — a >25x blowup purely from textual #include.
  - Implication: Never allow textual source inclusion; require a binary or structured, precompiled module-interface format (compiled once, imported many times) as the only mechanism for cross-file dependencies, eliminating the N-times-reparsing cost that dominates C/C++ build times.
  - Sources: https://bellard.org/tcc/ https://docs.carbon-lang.dev/toolchain/docs/

  - Evidence: Incremental compilation getting close to default for some cases; self-hosted x86_64/ELF backend and C backend have good incremental support; LLVM backend incremental support added April 2026.
  - Implication: Design incremental compilation as a property of the intermediate representation and dependency tracking (per-declaration/per-function granularity with content hashing), not as something tied to a specific fast backend — this lets even the optimizing/release backend benefit from incremental builds.
  - Sources: https://ziglang.org/devlog/2025/ https://github.com/ziglang/zig/issues/21165
- **V claims to compile at roughly 1.5 million lines of code per second per CPU core via direct machine-code generation, positioned explicitly against C-family compile speeds; this figure comes from the project's own marketing rather than an independently reproduced benchmark.** (low)
  - Evidence: V's stated claim: 1.5M LoC/s per core via direct machine code generation.
  - Implication: Treat vendor-stated LoC/sec numbers (V, Jai) as unverified marketing until reproduced on a public benchmark corpus; our own project should publish a reproducible, versioned compile-speed benchmark suite (fixed hardware spec, fixed source corpus, clean vs incremental) from day one to keep our own claims credible and comparable.
  - Sources: https://programming-language-benchmarks.vercel.app/v

Pitfalls:
- Tiered backends (fast debug + optimizing release) can silently diverge in semantics (integer representation, pointer provenance, inline-asm handling) unless the language spec pins down defined behavior precisely and CI runs differential tests between backends — Zig has hit this repeatedly.
- Unrestricted generics with full monomorphization can produce exponential compile-time/binary-size blowup in nested-generic code; don't make full monomorphization the only or default strategy.
- Rich, bidirectional type inference combined with overloading (Swift's mistake) can make type-checking worst-case exponential even for innocuous-looking code — avoid global constraint-solving type checkers.
- Parallelizing an incremental compiler's query system correctly (avoiding data races, keeping cycle-detection semantics consistent between parallel and serial paths) is still an open, multi-year problem for a team as large as rustc's in 2026 — don't underestimate this engineering cost.
- Vendor/marketing compile-speed claims (Jai, V) are largely unverified by independent reproduction; don't build our performance targets on unreproducible numbers.
- The linker can dominate build time as much as the frontend/codegen; a compiler that is fast internally but relies on a slow sequential linker (like parts of lld) will not deliver fast end-to-end builds.
- Textual #include-style dependency mechanisms cause superlinear reparse costs (25x+ line-count blowup observed in real C code) — any macro/include-like feature in a new language must not re-lex/re-parse shared text per translation unit.

## Survey: security

"Security built in" for a new language spans at least five layers: (1) a memory-safety type system that eliminates spatial/temporal/data-race bugs at compile time, (2) capability-based access control that removes ambient authority so libraries cannot silently touch files/network, (3) supply-chain controls (sandboxed builds, signed/reproducible artifacts, auditing) that address how real-world breaches (xz, npm, PyPI) actually happened, (4) elimination or tight scoping of undefined behavior, integer overflow, and uninitialized memory, with audited unsafe/FFI escape hatches, and (5) side-channel-aware primitives (constant-time/secret types) and optional formal-verification hooks for high-assurance code. Current state of the art shows real tradeoffs: Rust-style affine ownership gives zero-cost safety but has steep learning curves and still-incomplete flow-sensitivity (Polonius); Hylo's mutable value semantics and Vale's generational references trade a little compile-time complexity or 2-11% runtime overhead for much simpler mental models; Pony and Verona get compile-time data-race freedom via reference capabilities/regions with zero runtime sync cost; hardware capabilities (CHERI) and GC-based retrofits (Fil-C) show two very different ways to memory-safety existing C/C++ code. Capability security (WASI, Austral, Pony, Deno) is converging on "no ambient authority by default" as the supply-chain answer to attacks like xz and npm/PyPI takeovers, and CISA/NSA are now formally pushing memory-safe-language roadmaps as of 2025-2026, meaning government/regulatory pressure will treat memory safety as close to mandatory for new systems languages going forward.

- **Rust's flow-sensitive borrow checker successor (Polonius) is finally nearing stabilization in 2026, having taken over a decade to move from NLL to a scalable next-gen algorithm.** (high)
  - Evidence: Polonius Alpha (a superset of NLL that also handles lending-iterator patterns and NLL problem case #3) was enabled on nightly in Aug 2026 with no known remaining soundness issues, targeting stabilization in following months; full flow-sensitive Polonius remains future work.
  - Implication: Budget for borrow/alias-checker research to take 5-10+ years past v1.0 to mature; ship a deliberately conservative initial borrow model and plan an explicit, versioned upgrade path (like Rust's NLL->Polonius) rather than promising a 'final' checker at launch.
  - Sources: https://blog.rust-lang.org/2026/08/04/enabling-polonius-alpha-on-nightly/ https://rust-lang.github.io/polonius/current_status.html
- **Hylo achieves memory safety, type safety, and data-race freedom via Mutable Value Semantics (MVS) instead of Rust-style borrowing, arguing this is simpler because all possible accesses to a parameter are visible in the function body and cannot hide behind stored references.** (medium)
  - Evidence: Hylo's design papers state ordinary code is memory-safe/type-safe/data-race-free by construction under MVS, with a value-oriented model simpler than Rust's constrained-reference model or Swift's hybrid model.
  - Implication: Consider MVS (value semantics + implicit copy-on-write + in-place mutation) as the default aliasing model for ergonomic 'accidentally safe' code, reserving an explicit borrow/reference sublanguage only for performance-critical or generic code, to lower the safety learning curve versus pure Rust-style ownership.
  - Sources: https://hylo-lang.org/introduction/ https://hylo-lang.org/docs/reference/specification/
- **Vale's generational references give memory safety without GC or borrow-checking at a measured 2-10.84% runtime overhead, more than 2x faster than reference counting, with a planned region-borrow-checker feature to eliminate most of the remaining generation checks.** (medium)
  - Evidence: Every object carries a generation counter incremented on free; every reference carries a remembered generation; dereference asserts they match. Immutable-region borrowing lets the compiler skip most checks for a scope.
  - Implication: A generation-check + region-optimization scheme is a viable middle ground for a language wanting 'safe by default, fast by request': ship simple generational checks everywhere, then add an opt-in region/borrow layer that a compiler can prove eliminates checks in hot loops — this maps directly onto the 'fastest compiler' and 'most efficient' goals by making safety a progressively-optimizable cost rather than all-or-nothing.
  - Sources: https://verdagon.dev/blog/generational-references https://verdagon.dev/blog/zero-cost-memory-safety-regions-overview
- **Austral combines linear types (for safe manual memory/resource management with zero runtime overhead) with capability-based security so third-party dependencies can be statically restricted from touching resources like the filesystem or network unless handed an explicit capability value.** (high)
  - Evidence: Austral's linear types prevent double-free/use-after-free/double-fetch without a GC or borrow checker; 'linear capabilities' gate access to low-level facilities, explicitly framed by the designer as a supply-chain-attack mitigation.
  - Implication: Make capabilities first-class *values* (not just an OS/runtime permission list) threaded through function signatures — a library function that never receives a `FileCapability` parameter should be statically provable to never touch the filesystem, directly closing the class of npm/PyPI/xz-style 'a random dependency reached out to the network' attacks.
  - Sources: https://github.com/austral/austral https://borretti.me/article/introducing-austral
- **WASI/the WebAssembly Component Model's long-term security direction is 'zero ambient authority': a component instantiated with an empty context has no filesystem, network, clock, or RNG access at all, enforced at the runtime boundary rather than relying on the guest's cooperation.** (high)
  - Evidence: WASI Preview 2 is close to this goal — filesystem access is limited to explicit preopens; with none, the guest sees no host filesystem; this is explicitly contrasted with ambient-authority OS process models (Linux/Windows) where a process inherits the user's broad permissions.
  - Implication: Design the module/component boundary (not just the language's own type system) to be capability-safe: even compiled artifacts, plugins, or dynamically loaded code should get zero implicit access to host resources, so security holds even for untrusted/third-party compiled code, not just source reviewed at compile time.
  - Sources: https://wasi.dev/security https://www.systemshardening.com/articles/wasm/wasip3-security-roadmap/
- **Deno's sandboxed-by-default runtime shows the practical limits of capability/permission systems bolted onto an existing language: coarse flags like --allow-run and --allow-ffi are themselves 'escape hatches' that fully bypass the sandbox once granted.** (medium)
  - Evidence: Deno requires explicit --allow-read/-write/-net/-env/-run/-sys/-ffi/-import flags, most scopable to specific resources, but allow-run and allow-ffi are called out as ways to bypass the sandbox entirely.
  - Implication: Avoid coarse 'escape valve' permissions (raw FFI, raw subprocess spawn) that grant unrestricted authority; if FFI/exec must exist, require them to still go through capability objects (e.g., a `Subprocess` capability scoped to an argv allowlist) so no single flag reopens ambient authority.
  - Sources: https://docs.deno.com/runtime/fundamentals/security/ https://docs.deno.com/runtime/reference/permissions/
- **The xz-utils backdoor (CVE-2024-3094) succeeded specifically because the malicious payload existed only in the *build output* (autotools-generated files, conditionally triggered only on specific Debian/RPM-style build configurations), not in reviewed source, so source-level code review and casual CI verification both missed it.** (high)
  - Evidence: Analysis states the payload only existed in decoded form after build macros ran, and the backdoor activated only under specific build conditions (shared-lib build via gcc, x86-64 Linux, certain linker flags) uncommon in ad hoc dev/CI environments; maintainer takeover via years of social-engineered trust was the entry vector.
  - Implication: Design the build system itself to be part of the trusted-computing base: no Turing-complete, unsandboxed build-script/macro stage (a direct critique of autotools and of Rust build.rs/proc-macros running with full ambient authority); make the build reproducible and deterministic so 'what got compiled' is always re-derivable and diffable from source, and require build scripts to declare needed capabilities (network, filesystem writes) explicitly, sandboxed by default.
  - Sources: https://xygeni.io/blog/xz-backdoor-that-was-a-close-one/ https://cycode.com/blog/xz-backdoor-software-supply-chain-attack/
- **CISA, NSA, FBI and international partners have moved from guidance to a formal expectation: software manufacturers are pushed to publish a memory-safety roadmap by January 1, 2026, as part of the Secure-by-Design program, treating memory-safe languages or hardware-capability enforcement (e.g. CHERI) as the two accepted paths.** (high)
  - Evidence: CISA's June 2025 joint guide 'Memory Safe Languages: Reducing Vulnerabilities in Modern Software Development' and 'The Case for Memory Safe Roadmaps' (with NSA/FBI/Five Eyes partners) set this expectation explicitly.
  - Implication: Position the new language's memory-safety story (and any interop/migration path from C/C++) as directly answering this regulatory push — provide first-class tooling to generate the kind of 'memory-safety roadmap' evidence (e.g., static proof reports, unsafe-code inventories) that CISA-aligned enterprises will need to produce.
  - Sources: https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/alerts/2025/06/24/new-guidance-released-reducing-memory-related-vulnerabilities https://www.cisa.gov/case-memory-safe-roadmaps

  - Evidence: CHERI research (CPP 2025, arXiv papers on CHERI-D, PICASSO, VeriCHERI) covers both spatial and increasingly temporal (use-after-free) safety via hardware-enforced capabilities; Morello evaluation found ~1% of benchmark source needed changes.
  - Implication: Design the language's pointer/reference representation to be CHERI-capability-compatible (fat pointers with bounds+permissions+validity tag) so that on CHERI-enabled hardware the language's own safety checks can be partially or fully offloaded to hardware, reducing the runtime cost of the 'security built in' goal on future silicon.

- **Fil-C demonstrates an alternative retrofit path to memory safety for existing unsafe C/C++ semantics: complete memory safety with zero escape hatches, achieved via a concurrent garbage collector plus 'InvisiCaps' (invisible per-pointer capabilities) built on a Clang/LLVM fork, with most real-world C/C++ software compiling with zero or minimal source changes.** (medium)
  - Evidence: Fil-C is built on Clang 20.1.8 supporting C17/C++20; all possibly-unsafe operations are checked at runtime and violations become Fil-C panics rather than UB; the project explicitly claims 'zero escape hatches.'
  - Implication: For a new language wanting strong FFI compatibility with existing C/C++ codebases, evaluate a Fil-C-style GC+capability retrofit as the *FFI boundary* strategy — untrusted or unaudited C libraries linked in could be compiled through such a safety layer rather than trusted at face value, shrinking the unsafe-FFI attack surface without demanding a full rewrite.
  - Sources: https://fil-c.org/how https://github.com/pizlonator/fil-c/
- **Pony achieves data-race-free actor-model concurrency at zero runtime cost — no locks, atomics, or synchronization primitives anywhere in its standard library — purely through static reference-capability analysis, and its actor-local GC never needs a global stop-the-world pause because cross-actor reference cycles are ruled out by the type system.** (high)
  - Evidence: Reference capabilities are checked statically; cross-actor mutable data passing is proven race-free at compile time; standard library has zero locks; as of Oct 2025 Pony 0.60.0 shipped with improved multi-arch container support, indicating active maintenance.
  - Implication: For the 'fastest parallelization' goal, adopt a reference-capability system (isolated/mutable-exclusive/immutable-shared/read-only categories checked at compile time) as the concurrency model rather than runtime locks or even Rust's Send/Sync marker traits — this is the most direct known technique for compile-time-provable, zero-overhead data-race freedom across many cores/actors.
  - Sources: https://www.javacodegeeks.com/2026/09/ponys-reference-capabilities-data-race-free-actors-without-locks-or-gc-pauses.html https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pony_(programming_language)
- **Swift 6's strict concurrency (Sendable protocol + compiler-enforced actor isolation) shows the retrofit difficulty of adding compile-time data-race safety to an existing large-ecosystem language: even with 'complete concurrency checking' the compiler still tracks values crossing actor boundaries rather than requiring every type be marked, and critics note some real data races still slip past the Swift 6 checker.** (medium)
  - Evidence: Swift 6 makes data-race safety checks (previously opt-in warnings via -strict-concurrency=complete since 5.10) into default, project-wide enforcement; noncopyable (~Copyable) types were added for RAII-style resource safety; independent analysis ('Data Races Swift 6 Still Can't Catch') documents gaps.
  - Implication: Do not assume a bolted-on Sendable-style marker-trait system is sufficient for provable data-race freedom — prefer capability/region-based concurrency designed in from day one (per Pony/Verona) over an additive annotation system, since retrofit annotation systems demonstrably have soundness gaps even after years of iteration.
  - Sources: https://www.swift.org/blog/announcing-swift-6/ https://bleepingswift.com/blog/data-races-swift-6-cant-catch
- **Miri, Rust's dynamic Undefined-Behavior interpreter for unsafe code, is the first practical tool (in any language) that can reliably detect nearly all de-facto UB in deterministic programs, requires zero source annotation, ships with the nightly toolchain, and has found many real bugs in production unsafe Rust — but it cannot systematically explore concurrent interleavings.** (high)
  - Evidence: POPL 2026 paper 'Miri: Practical Undefined Behavior Detection for Rust' describes it as trivial to deploy and effective at dynamic UB detection, with the caveat that it is dynamic (path-dependent) and weak on concurrency-interleaving coverage.
  - Implication: Plan a Miri-equivalent interpreter/sanitizer as a first-class, officially maintained tool from the start (not an ecosystem afterthought) for validating any `unsafe`-equivalent escape hatch — this is what actually lets an 'unsafe' block be audited rather than just trusted, and is a precondition for CISA-style memory-safety-roadmap evidence.

- **Ada/SPARK demonstrates a mature, industrially deployed contracts-and-refinement-types approach: preconditions/postconditions/invariants embedded as logical specs let the GNATprove tool automatically discharge proofs of safety, functional correctness, and termination for a systems language, without requiring full interactive theorem proving for most code.** (medium)
  - Evidence: SPARK contracts enable modular, per-subprogram reasoning; GNATprove automates proof discharge for absence of runtime errors and functional correctness; this has decades of use in aerospace/defense.
  - Implication: Design contracts (pre/post/invariant) as a built-in, first-class language feature (not a library or external annotation format) with an SMT-backed prover integrated into the standard toolchain, so 'formal verification hooks' are a gradient from 'runtime-checked contract' to 'statically proved contract' rather than a separate tool users must adopt (Verus/Dafny-style) after the fact.
  - Sources: https://iccl.inf.tu-dresden.de/web/Software_Verification_in_Ada/SPARK/en https://popl26.sigplan.org/details/dafny-2026-papers/13/A-benchmark-for-vericoding-formally-verified-program-synthesis
- **Verus and Dafny represent two different verification-language architectures: Verus embeds ghost (spec-only) types alongside native Rust execution types and must reason about machine-level details like integer overflow, while Dafny uses uniform mathematical types abstracted away from machine representation — a tradeoff between verification convenience and fidelity to actual runtime behavior.** (medium)
  - Evidence: Comparative analysis of Dafny vs. Verus notes Dafny's uniform math types versus Verus's dual ghost/native-type system requiring overflow-aware verification.
  - Implication: If the language wants verification hooks that prove properties about the *actual compiled/parallelized* code (needed for a 'fastest compute parallelization' claim to also be provably safe), follow Verus's approach of verifying against real machine-level types (including overflow, bit-width) rather than Dafny's idealized math types — otherwise proofs won't cover the overflow/UB bugs that matter for a systems language.
  - Sources: https://popl26.sigplan.org/details/dafny-2026-papers/13/A-benchmark-for-vericoding-formally-verified-program-synthesis https://arxiv.org/html/2605.03822v1
- **Constant-time/secret-type protections against cache-timing side channels are increasingly defeated by Spectre-class transient execution, requiring compiler-level taint tracking and instruction-selection changes (e.g., the Serberus/LLSCT approach) rather than just source-level 'constant-time coding style,' because speculative execution can steer secret data into cache/timing-observable channels even when the architectural (non-speculative) control/data flow is constant-time.** (high)
  - Evidence: Serberus (LLSCT compiler) performs static taint analysis to identify secret data and applies mitigations producing more performant and secure binaries than prior state-of-the-art for cryptographic routines; the constant-time discipline alone was shown insufficient against speculative/transient leaks.
  - Implication: If offering a `secret<T>`/constant-time type qualifier, its compiler enforcement must include speculative-taint-tracking and instruction-selection constraints (à la Serberus), not merely a ban on secret-dependent branches/memory-indices at the source level — a source-only constant-time type system gives false confidence against Spectre-class leaks.
  - Sources: https://trippel-lab.stanford.edu/pubs/mosier_SP24.pdf https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.05174
- **Rust ecosystem supply-chain tooling has converged on a layered defense: cargo-vet (Mozilla's human-audit-required gate for third-party crates, delegable to trusted orgs' published audit sets), cargo-audit/cargo-deny in CI, official SBOM generation (cargo-cyclonedx/cargo-sbom), and emerging Sigstore-based crate signing for SLSA-aligned provenance — none of which is built into the language/package manager by default today.** (medium)
  - Evidence: Community checklist: cargo audit + cargo deny in CI, cargo-vet policy/audits for critical crates, SBOM artifacts via cargo-cyclonedx, and a pre-RFC proposing sigstore-based crate signing as still-pending (not yet standard).
  - Implication: Bake supply-chain trust primitives into the package manager from day one rather than as bolt-on third-party tools: mandatory build-script sandboxing (capability-scoped), signed+reproducible package builds by default, and a built-in audit-ledger (cargo-vet-like) as a first-class package-manager feature — this closes the gap that Rust/npm/PyPI all still have as optional add-ons years into their ecosystems' lives.
  - Sources: https://www.systemshardening.com/articles/cicd/rust-cargo-supply-chain-security/ https://rust-secure-code.github.io/rust-supply-chain-security/build.html

Pitfalls:
- Treating memory safety as solved by a single mechanism (borrow checking alone, or GC alone) — real state of the art shows multiple complementary layers (type-level ownership, capability security, hardware assists, dynamic UB detectors) are needed together.
- Underestimating how long a sound, ergonomic borrow/alias checker takes to mature — Rust took over a decade from NLL to Polonius Alpha and full flow-sensitivity is still future work.
- Adding data-race-safety as a retrofit annotation system (Swift 6 Sendable-style) after the core language is designed — this reliably leaves soundness gaps versus designing capability/region-based concurrency in from the start.
- Assuming source-level 'constant-time' code is sufficient against side channels — Spectre-class transient execution defeats naive constant-time discipline and requires compiler-level speculative-taint tracking.
- Leaving supply-chain security (build sandboxing, signing, SBOM, audit) as optional third-party tooling rather than a package-manager default — this is exactly the gap that let attacks like xz-utils and npm/PyPI takeovers succeed despite source code being 'reviewable.'
- Providing coarse all-or-nothing escape-hatch permissions (unrestricted FFI/exec, an `unsafe` block with no audit tooling) that fully bypass capability sandboxes or safety guarantees once granted — as seen with Deno's --allow-run/--allow-ffi and generic 'unsafe' blocks without a Miri-equivalent auditor.
- Ignoring that memory-safety runtime cost is not free even in state-of-the-art designs (Vale's 2-11% overhead, GC pause avoidance tradeoffs) — a 'most efficient language in existence' claim must reconcile with these known costs or justify why a specific technique (e.g., region-optimized generational refs, CHERI hardware offload) avoids them.

## Survey: parallelism

The state of the art in parallel-compute languages splits into three lanes that no single system unifies well: (1) safety-by-construction concurrency (Rust Send/Sync, Pony reference capabilities, Swift 6 Sendable/actors) which eliminates data races at compile time but each is scoped to a single memory model (shared-memory Rust, actor-only Pony, app-framework Swift); (2) algorithm/schedule-separated compilers (Halide, TVM/Ansor, Triton, MLIR linalg/affine) that let autoschedulers or ILP-based polyhedral analysis reach 90-100%+ of hand-tuned performance, but only within restricted, mostly affine/tensor domains; (3) distributed/heterogeneous task runtimes (Chapel, Legion/Regent, Kokkos/RAJA) that hit near-native performance across CPU/GPU/cluster but require heavyweight runtimes and are not integrated with a race-free type system. Mojo (now Apache-2.0 open source as of August 2026) is the most direct attempt to build a new general-purpose language on MLIR for portable heterogeneous performance, but published results show it still trails CUDA/HIP on compute-bound and atomic-heavy kernels. Bend/HVM2 is the most radical structural departure — automatic massive parallelism from interaction nets with near-ideal scaling to 10,000+ cores — but at a steep single-core performance cost, showing that "free" parallelism and raw throughput are currently in tension. Zig's 2026 colorless async I/O (std.Io) shows a orthogonal, adoptable pattern: decouple the concurrency mechanism from the call site via an interface parameter, avoiding function-coloring while still enabling io_uring-level efficiency. HPCG/HPL leadership in 2026 (LineShine, ~2.2 Exaflop/s) remains dominated by custom silicon/interconnect and hand-tuned MPI+X, not by a new language — reinforcing that a language-level win must come from compiler/runtime co-design, not asking programmers to hand-tune less.

- **Rayon (Rust) work-stealing has real, measurable overhead from split/steal granularity and generalist scheduling; task-based alternatives with Cilk-style spawn/sync (e.g. Velvet) match or beat it on up to 128 cores.** (high)
  - Evidence: Velvet study: 'adopts a Cilk-style spawn-sync abstraction with work-stealing scheduling, and achieves competitive performance and scalability with state-of-the-art Rust and C frameworks, namely Rayon, Cilk and OpenMP, on up to 128 cores.' Separately: Rayon's work stealing 'doesn't go down to the granularity of individual items... splits the input slice into a tree of jobs' incurring overhead; a case study got a 10x speedup by working around Rayon's generalist design.
  - Implication: Our language's parallel runtime should expose a low-level Cilk-style fork/join primitive (cheap, provably low-overhead) as the compilation target, and let higher-level combinators (par-iter, par-map) lower to it — so the compiler, not a generic library, controls grain size and steal granularity per call site.
  - Sources: https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-032-35248-4_3 https://gendignoux.com/blog/2024/11/18/rust-rayon-optimized.html
- **Data-race freedom enforced by the type system (Rust Send/Sync, Pony reference capabilities, Swift 6 Sendable/actor isolation) is now mainstream and can be a compile-time-only cost — but each existing design is bound to one concurrency model.** (high)
  - Evidence: Pony: 'reference capabilities... let a pointer to real, unmodified memory cross from one actor to another, and prove at compile time that doing so can never create a race,' with no locks, no stop-the-world GC. Swift 6: 'strict concurrency checking eliminates data races at compile time... Eliminating data races allows the compiler to make more aggressive optimizations.' Rust's Send/Sync are marker traits with zero runtime cost, checked entirely at compile time.
  - Implication: Design a unified capability/permission system (ownership + isolation domains) that covers shared-memory threads, actors, AND GPU kernels in one type-level model, rather than bolting on a separate race-free story per execution mode — this is a genuine structural gap current languages have (Rust doesn't cover actors/GPU; Pony doesn't cover GPU/SIMD; Swift is app-framework-bound).
  - Sources: https://www.javacodegeeks.com/2026/09/ponys-reference-capabilities-data-race-free-actors-without-locks-or-gc-pauses.html https://www.hackingwithswift.com/swift/6.0/concurrency
- **ISPC's explicit SPMD-on-SIMD model is more predictable than compiler auto-vectorization, which is brittle under control-flow divergence and silently falls back to scalar code.** (high)
  - Evidence: 'Auto-vectorization is a complex optimization full of heuristics... auto-vectorizers are doomed to be brittle and unpredictable... A key drawback of auto-vectorization is that it can easily break when control flow diverges and revert to fully scalar code. In contrast, ISPC... will still vectorize the code, though at the cost of introducing control flow masking.'
  - Implication: Do not rely solely on auto-vectorization for the performance story; give programmers (or the compiler's own kernel-generation pass) an explicit SPMD execution mode with guaranteed-vectorized semantics and predictable masked control flow, so vector width is a compile-time contract, not a heuristic outcome.
  - Sources: https://en.algorithmica.org/hpc/simd/auto-vectorization/ https://pharr.org/matt/blog/2018/04/21/ispc-volta-c-and-spmd

  - Evidence: 'With appropriate architecture and runtime support, the Chapel model can achieve performance equal to the best Fortran/MPI, Co-Array Fortran, and OpenMP implementations, while substantially easing the burden on the application code developer.'   - Implication: Adopt a PGAS-style locale/domain-map abstraction as a first-class distributed array/data-parallel model, but budget significant engineering for the runtime (communication scheduling, data layout) since the productivity win is real but performance parity is not free.
  - Sources: https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3528425.3529104 https://blog.lambdaclass.com/interview-with-brad-chamberlain-about-chapel-a-productive-parallel-programming-language/
- **Legion/Regent's logical-regions + implicit-parallelism model lets a single program run unmodified across single-CPU, single-GPU, and multi-node heterogeneous clusters, with the runtime handling data movement, and Regent's compiler-generated code matches hand-tuned Legion.** (high)
  - Evidence: 'Regent achieves performance comparable to hand-tuned Legion.' 'Applications written in Regent can be executed on various system configurations... with virtually no changes.' Real-world results: 'up to 62.5X speedup on 96 GPUs on the Keeneland supercomputer.'
  - Implication: A logical-region/partitioning abstraction (data described independent of placement, with an explicit mapping interface) is a proven way to get 'write once, run on CPU/GPU/cluster' — our language should separate the *what* (task/data dependencies) from the *where* (mapper/scheduler), analogous to Halide's algorithm/schedule split but for distributed heterogeneous systems.

  - Evidence: 'The Affine dialect... imposing a restrictive but powerful structural constraint: all control flow and memory accesses must be defined using affine (linear) functions...   - Implication: Design our IR as a stack of progressively-lowering dialects like MLIR (high-level structured/tensor ops -> affine loops -> low-level), so easy, provably-legal transformations happen at the highest applicable level and only fall back to general polyhedral analysis (expensive, general) when necessary — this directly targets 'fastest compiler' by avoiding unnecessary heavyweight analysis.

  - Evidence: 'Kotlin introduces coroutines as a lightweight, structured concurrency model... unlike Java which relies on threads, locks, and atomic operations.' 'Current implementations use coroutines and ignore OS threads, which means they can't use structured concurrency on more than one CPU core.'
  - Implication: Extend structured concurrency (scoped lifetimes, automatic cancellation/error propagation) to be the native model for both single-core async tasks AND multi-core/distributed parallel tasks, unifying what Trio/Kotlin only do for concurrency with what Cilk/Rayon do for parallelism — this is a concrete gap: no mainstream language has structured concurrency spanning both concerns uniformly.
  - Sources: https://grokipedia.com/page/Structured_concurrency https://elizarov.medium.com/structured-concurrency-722d765aa952
- **OpenCilk/Tapir demonstrates that fork-join parallelism can be represented directly in the compiler's IR (not just as a library call), enabling the optimizer to reason about and optimize across parallel control flow — and researchers have prototyped bringing this reasoning into Rust's compiler.** (medium)
  - Evidence: MIT thesis: 'Enabling the Rust Compiler to Reason about Fork/Join Parallelism via Tapir'; found 'Rust + Cilk exhibits marginally worse performance than Rayon, although differences are possible to bridge with further work.'
  - Implication: Represent fork/join and parallel-for constructs as first-class IR primitives (Tapir-style spawn/sync instructions) from day one, so standard compiler optimizations (inlining, vectorization, dead-code elimination) can see through and across parallel regions instead of treating them as opaque library/runtime calls — this is what makes a 'fastest compiler for parallel code' claim credible.
  - Sources: https://dspace.mit.edu/entities/publication/e8554bdb-2cb4-4c0e-b44b-9f605da06a6f https://cilk.mit.edu/

Pitfalls:
- Assuming automatic/implicit parallelism (interaction nets, Bend/HVM2) is a free lunch: it currently sacrifices substantial single-core performance, so 'most efficient language in existence' and 'fastest parallelization' can directly conflict unless both an implicit and an explicit low-overhead mode are offered.
- Betting compiler speed entirely on general polyhedral analysis: full polyhedral (ILP-based) dependence analysis is powerful but potentially expensive; MLIR's linalg dialect approach shows it's better to avoid it when higher-level structure already guarantees legality.
- Treating performance-portability abstraction layers (Kokkos/RAJA-style) as inherently free — published data shows 5-30% overhead versus hand-tuned code is common unless backend-specific specialization is aggressively pursued.
- Conflating concurrency (task interleaving, e.g. async/await, actors) with parallelism (simultaneous execution across cores) — most 'structured concurrency' implementations (Trio, Kotlin coroutines) are single-core and do not solve multi-core parallel scheduling; do not market one as solving the other.
- Chasing raw HPL/HPCG top-of-TOP500 supremacy as a benchmark target — that ranking is presently won by custom silicon/interconnect/OS co-design (e.g., LineShine), not language choice; a new language cannot credibly claim to beat that on hardware it doesn't control.
- Building a new race-free type system without covering GPU/accelerator memory models — existing systems (Rust, Pony, Swift) each cover only one execution domain (shared-memory threads, actors, or app frameworks respectively), leaving GPU/heterogeneous races as an unsolved compile-time-checkable problem.
- Underestimating engineering cost of a Chapel/Legion-style runtime: productivity and portability gains are real but historically required years of dedicated runtime/compiler co-design to reach hand-tuned-MPI parity.

## Survey: bootstrapping-strategy

Every successful new systems language followed the same skeleton: build a throwaway bootstrap compiler in a productive, already-available language (OCaml, C, C++, Swift, Rust), reach self-hosting as a forcing function for dogfooding, then optionally rewrite backends/tooling once semantics stabilize. LLVM is the default backend for reaching credible codegen quickly, but every project citing "fastest compiler" (Zig, Roc, Odin) has either moved off LLVM for debug builds or explicitly named LLVM as their compile-time bottleneck — meaning a from-scratch "fastest compiler" language cannot use LLVM alone as its story. Adoption levers that actually mattered are C ABI/FFI compatibility and header-import tooling (Zig's translate-c, Carbon's C++ interop, Mojo's Python interop), not language elegance. Tooling-from-day-one (LSP via incremental query architecture like rust-analyzer/salsa, formatter, package manager) determined whether early adopters stuck around. Specification rigor varies enormously — Austral shipped a 100-page spec and a <600-line borrow checker deliberately to stay implementable by one person, while Carbon and Rust let the spec trail the implementation. Team size and funding correlate strongly with speed to 1.0: Go (small Google team, ~2 years to open source), Rust (Mozilla-funded team, ~9 years design-to-1.0), Zig (near-solo/small-foundation, still pre-1.0 after 10 years by design), V (solo, overpromised, credibility damage), Gleam (solo creator, sponsor-funded full-time). The clearest failure mode across V, Vale, and various hobby languages is publicly promising performance/feature numbers or a novel unproven safety mechanism before an independently reproducible result exists — the most reputation-costly and time-costly mistake for a new low-level language claiming to be "the fastest" and "secure by design."

- **Rust bootstrapped through a throwaway OCaml compiler (rustboot) before self-hosting; self-hosting was reached in about one year and the OCaml compiler was then fully discarded.** (high)
  - Evidence: rustboot (2010) was written in OCaml specifically to build a second, Rust-written compiler (rustc) and begin self-hosting; Rust first built itself on 2011-04-20 (took 1 hour); by 2011 the OCaml dependency was gone and rustc used LLVM as codegen backend instead of rustboot's hand-written x86 codegen.
  - Implication: Plan the bootstrap compiler as intentionally disposable: write it in whatever language lets you iterate fastest on semantics, target self-hosting as an early milestone, and budget for discarding the bootstrap implementation once the self-hosted compiler compiles itself.
  - Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rust_(programming_language) https://www.languagelineage.org/languages/rust
- **Zig's bootstrap chain went C++ to a WASI-interpreted Zig-to-C transpile to fully self-hosted, specifically to avoid needing any pre-existing Zig binary or a complex multi-platform bootstrap matrix.** (high)
  - Evidence: Stage0 uses a from-scratch WASI interpreter (built from C) to run a WASM blob of the self-hosted Zig compiler compiled down to C, which a system C compiler then builds — a single portable WASM blob avoids needing per-platform prebuilt binaries. The original C++ implementation was fully deleted once self-hosting was solid.
  - Implication: If cross-platform bootstrap-from-source matters, design the bootstrap artifact as a portable IR compiled through a minimal from-scratch interpreter in C, rather than shipping N platform-specific bootstrap binaries.
  - Sources: https://ziglang.org/news/goodbye-cpp/ https://github.com/ziglang/zig/issues/853
- **Go was deliberately bootstrapped in C — not a 'modern' language — for pragmatic reasons, then the C compiler was mechanically translated to Go six years after Go's public release.** (high)
  - Evidence: Gc was originally written in C because of the difficulties of bootstrapping; Russ Cox built a mechanical C-to-Go translator in 2013, becoming the foundation of Go 1.5's self-hosted compiler (Aug 2015), about 6 years after Go's 2009 open-source release.
  - Implication: Self-hosting doesn't need to happen immediately — Go shipped for 6 years pre-1.5 without a self-hosted compiler and still gained major adoption. Treat self-hosting as a later maturity milestone, and consider automated source-to-source transpilation as a shortcut once feasible.
  - Sources: https://go.dev/doc/faq https://www.languagelineage.org/languages/go
- **Roc rewrote its entire compiler from Rust to Zig, citing Cargo incremental build times (3.4s in Rust vs 35ms in Zig), architectural problems with lambda-set resolution, and difficulty getting Rust to emit the exact LLVM bitcode needed for whole-program optimization.** (high)
  - Evidence: rtfeldman.com/rust-to-zig describes a 487-day, ~300K-line rewrite; the team wanted simpler, more consistent designs prioritizing maintainability/correctness, and found Zig's allocator-centric memory model aligned better with Roc's needs.
  - Implication: Weight incremental-build latency and control over backend/IR integration heavily when choosing our own compiler's implementation language — a slow edit-compile-test loop for the compiler team compounds across years and can force a costly full rewrite.
  - Sources: https://rtfeldman.com/rust-to-zig https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=42935516
- **Carbon's core adoption strategy is migration-first, not safety-first: bidirectional, near-zero-cost C++ interop comes first, with a staged, semi-automated migration path toward memory safety added later — the opposite order from Rust.** (high)
  - Evidence: 'Rust starts with safety and Carbon starts with migration' — Carbon plans automated migration from C++ into a Carbon dialect designed for C++ interop, then incremental refactoring toward memory-safe code, led by Chandler Carruth (Google), publicly announced July 2022.
  - Implication: Decide explicitly whether FFI/interop or safety is the adoption spearhead. We should invert Carbon's order (safety must not be deferred) while still borrowing its investment in bidirectional C ABI/header-import tooling as a day-one adoption lever.
  - Sources: https://github.com/carbon-language/carbon-lang https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_(programming_language)
- **Mojo built its compiler pipeline entirely on MLIR from day one (the first language fully built on MLIR) to target CPUs, GPUs, and future TPUs from one IR, and used CPython-runtime interop plus C-compatible bindings as its adoption bridge into the Python ecosystem.** (high)
  - Evidence: Modular Inc. (Chris Lattner + Tim Davis, founded 2022); Mojo uses a Python-superset syntax over an MLIR pipeline giving zero-cost abstractions, ownership-based memory management, and automatic vectorization; Python interop automatically converts lists/arrays/scalars via the CPython runtime.
  - Implication: For a language targeting 'fastest compute parallelization,' adopt an MLIR-based, multi-level, dialect-extensible IR from the start rather than a single fixed IR — the concrete mechanism used to target heterogeneous hardware without forking the compiler per backend.
  - Sources: https://docs.modular.com/mojo/vision/ https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mojo_(programming_language)
- **Hylo (formerly Val) is implemented in Swift, not self-hosted, and its design methodology was reductive: start from an existing mature language and strip it to a single organizing principle (mutable value semantics) to get provable memory/type/data-race safety without a Rust-style borrow checker.** (high)
  - Evidence: 'We took Swift, removed classes, and went from there' — Hylo requires Swift 6.2+ to build; the goal is the safety and local reasoning of pure functional programming while still allowing in-place mutation, positioned as simpler than Rust's reference model.
  - Implication: A credible alternate route to memory safety exists beyond Rust's borrow checker: value-semantics-based designs can be simpler to implement and teach. Worth formally evaluating mutable value semantics vs. an ownership/borrow-checker model.
  - Sources: https://hylo-lang.org/introduction/ https://github.com/hylo-lang/Documentation/blob/main/val-for-swift-users.md
- **Austral was explicitly scoped to be implementable and hold entirely 'in the mind of a single programmer,' resulting in a ~100-page spec and a borrow-checker equivalent under 600 lines of code, using linear types plus capability-based security instead of a full borrow checker.** (high)
  - Evidence: austral-lang.org/spec; linear types prevent double-free/use-after-free/double-fetch without GC overhead; capabilities are unforgeable, destroyable, non-duplicable permission tokens restricting what dependencies can access.
  - Implication: Deliberately bounding language complexity is a proven strategy for solo/small-team projects. If 'security built-in' includes supply-chain concerns, capability-based security is a concrete, implementable primitive worth adopting.
  - Sources: https://austral-lang.org/spec/spec.html https://borretti.me/article/introducing-austral
- **Vale pursued a novel memory-safety mechanism (generational references, later region borrow checking) as a largely solo project; per its own public roadmap the project has stalled mid-roadmap (still pre-1.0) while exploring further redesigns.** (medium)
  - Evidence: vale.dev/roadmap lists v0.3 (Summer 2023) and v0.6.1 (Early 2025) targets; verdagon.dev/blog/golden-spike-reviving-vale-valen indicates the project went dormant and is being revived/renamed; development is 'currently on hold while exploring... new memory safety blends.'
  - Implication: A solo-maintained language pursuing a genuinely novel, unproven memory-safety technique risks years of research-grade redesign before reaching 1.0. If we invent a novel safety mechanism, budget for multi-year uncertainty and keep a fallback design ready.
  - Sources: https://vale.dev/roadmap https://verdagon.dev/blog/golden-spike-reviving-vale-valen
- **The V language (2019) suffered lasting reputational damage from publishing benchmark and feature/release claims that were not independently reproducible at the time, prompting sustained 'vaporware' accusations even after the language shipped working software.** (medium)
  - Evidence: Reporting characterizes the pattern as 'overpromise and underdeliver'; detractors called V 'fake'/'vaporware' during its early period; disputes eventually settled but V retains a comparatively small community relative to its early hype.
  - Implication: Never publish comparative performance or 'fastest X' claims before there is a reproducible, third-party-runnable benchmark suite in the repo. Publish only measured, reproducible numbers with stated methodology.
  - Sources: https://justinas.org/the-bizarre-world-of-v https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V_(programming_language)
- **rust-analyzer uses a query-based, salsa-style incremental architecture as the concrete mechanism for delivering IDE/LSP support without bolting it on after the fact: compilation is modeled as memoized, dependency-tracked queries, with the invariant that editing inside a function body never invalidates global derived data.** (high)
  - Evidence: rust-analyzer is incremental per-file (vertically) and lazy across the whole crate graph (horizontally), unlike rustc's per-crate batch model; core crates deliberately maintain this invariant from the start.
  - Implication: Architect our compiler's core (name resolution, type checking) as an incremental, on-demand query system from the very first working version — reusable both for our 'fastest compiler' goal (incremental compiles) and for LSP-from-day-one.
  - Sources: https://rust-analyzer.github.io/book/contributing/architecture.html https://rust-analyzer.github.io/blog/2023/07/24/durable-incrementality.html
- **Zig's stated reason for building custom, non-LLVM backends is that LLVM itself was the dominant compile-time bottleneck; the self-hosted x86_64 backend cut a hello-world compile from 22.8s to 275ms and cut the time for the Zig compiler to compile itself from 75s to 20s, and its codegen can run in parallel with other compiler passes.** (high)
  - Evidence: 'the only way to improve compilation speed was for Zig to have its own custom backends'; the self-hosted x86_64 backend was turned on by default for Debug builds on Linux/macOS, with reported 5-70% wall-clock improvements depending on project size.
  - Implication: To credibly claim 'fastest compiler,' LLVM cannot be the sole/default backend for iterative dev builds — plan a custom, lighter-weight codegen backend for debug/dev builds, reserving LLVM/MLIR for optimized release builds.
  - Sources: https://mitchellh.com/writing/zig-builds-getting-faster https://biggo.com/news/202506090712_Zig_x86_Backend_Performance_Boost
- **Csmith-style random program generation plus differential testing across compilers/optimization levels is the proven method for finding miscompilation bugs (481 bugs, ~120 miscompilations found across LLVM/GCC/CompCert by 2013); modern Rust-specific tools (RustSmith, Rustlantis) apply the same idea at the MIR level, with bugs getting exponentially harder to find as a compiler matures.** (high)
  - Evidence: Rustlantis generates MIR directly via a mir!() macro and outperforms AST-level RustSmith at finding new bugs; Miri (the reference interpreter shared with rustc's constant-propagation/GVN passes) enables differential testing between interpreted and compiled semantics; mature compilers often need >100,000 generated programs to surface one new bug.
  - Implication: Build a Miri-like reference interpreter for our IR alongside the optimizing compiler early on, and add a random program/IR generator (Csmith-for-us) to CI before claiming compiler correctness, not after users hit miscompilations.
  - Sources: https://research.ralfj.de/papers/2024-oopsla-rustlantis.pdf https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.06584
- **Both Zig's translate-c/@cImport and Carbon's C++ interop treat 'call existing C/C++ code with zero or near-zero glue code' as the primary adoption lever, rather than relying on the new language's intrinsic merits alone.** (high)
  - Evidence: @cImport mechanically parses C headers into Zig types with no bindings generators, no FFI libraries, and no runtime overhead, because Zig kept C's memory model and calling conventions; Carbon's stated primary design goal is seamless bidirectional interoperability with C++.
  - Implication: Invest early in a C-header-import tool (parse .h files directly into our type system, no manual bindings) as a first-class adoption feature — the actual on-ramp used by every recent successful systems language.
  - Sources: https://deepwiki.com/ziglang/translate-c/7-usage-guide https://github.com/carbon-language/carbon-lang
- **Bend achieves automatic, annotation-free parallelism by compiling high-level functional code to an interaction-net-based massively parallel runtime (HVM), currently limited to NVIDIA GPUs only, and is presented as a working example of code that parallelizes without explicit thread/lock annotations as of 2026.** (medium)
  - Evidence: github.com/HigherOrderCO/Bend describes it as a 'massively parallel, high-level programming language' with near-linear acceleration by core count without explicit parallel primitives, currently NVIDIA-only for GPU support.
  - Implication: An interaction-net / optimal-reduction evaluation model is a concrete, working alternative to manual thread/task parallelism for a 'fastest parallelization' goal — worth evaluating for a pure/functional subset of our language, while recognizing current vendor-breadth and imperative-code tradeoffs.
  - Sources: https://github.com/HigherOrderCO/Bend
- **Chapel, a multi-decade institutionally backed project still actively releasing as of June 2026 (v2.9), demonstrates that unifying single-node and distributed-supercomputer parallelism plus vendor-neutral GPU support in one language is achievable, but only after a very long institutional-funding timeline.** (medium)
  - Evidence: Chapel supports vendor-neutral GPU programming using the same language features used for distributed execution; steady release cadence (2.9 as of June 2026) after originating in the 2000s DARPA HPCS program.
  - Implication: Distributed/heterogeneous-hardware parallelism as a core language feature has decades-scale institutional precedent; a solo/small team should scope 'fastest compute parallelization' down initially to single-node multi-core plus single-vendor GPU rather than Chapel's full distributed-HPC scope.
  - Sources: https://chapel-lang.org/
- **Gleam is a solo-founder (Louis Pilfold) language implemented in Rust (not self-hosted), sustained financially through direct sponsorship/GitHub Sponsors enabling full-time solo development, and reuses the mature BEAM/Erlang runtime rather than building a new execution engine from scratch.** (medium)
  - Evidence: 'Louis is working full-time to improve the language... thanks to sponsors'; Gleam compiles to Erlang/JavaScript, reusing BEAM's concurrency model and interoperating with existing Erlang/Elixir libraries.
  - Implication: Reusing a mature, battle-tested runtime is a legitimate way to shortcut runtime/concurrency engineering, though it caps backend control; for a native-code 'most efficient' language we likely cannot do this, but the sponsorship-funded solo-founder funding model is realistic to plan around if this stays a small/solo effort.
  - Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gleam_(programming_language)

Pitfalls:
- Announcing performance, safety, or timeline claims before they are independently reproducible (V-lang: lasting 'vaporware' reputation despite eventually shipping).
- Chasing a genuinely novel, unproven safety/memory mechanism as a solo project without a fallback design — risks multi-year 'perpetual redesign' limbo (Vale's stalled roadmap).
- Deferring self-hosting is fine, but deferring tooling (LSP, formatter, package manager) is not — retrofitting an incremental/query architecture after an initial batch-style compiler design is expensive.
- Relying solely on LLVM for all build modes forecloses any credible 'fastest compiler' claim — LLVM's own compile-time overhead has been explicitly cited as the bottleneck by Zig and Roc.
- Underestimating platform ABI work: even with LLVM doing codegen, ABI handling still has to be manually implemented per target — easy to underscope.
- Scope creep / unbounded design ambition without a hard complexity budget (contrast Austral's deliberate 'fits in one programmer's head' constraint with more open-ended projects that drift for years).
